# ILAC Legal Report + Discussion (COM741 Media Law & Regulations)

Use this skill when NIK asks for an ILAC analysis, a legal report, a case report on a judgment, or an analysis of a media/news incident (an arrest over a post, an MCMC takedown, a publication ban, a defamation suit, etc.). Malaysia is the anchor jurisdiction. The reader is a postgraduate communication scholar, not a lawyer.

This is legal information for study, not legal advice. End every report with a one-line note saying so.

## What ILAC is

- **I — Issue**: the precise legal question, framed as a question.
- **L — Law**: the rules that answer it (Constitution, statute + section, case law, regulator codes).
- **A — Application**: apply each element of the law to the facts, arguing both sides where contested.
- **C — Conclusion**: a direct answer to the Issue, with the degree of certainty.

One ILAC per issue. If a report raises three issues, write three ILAC blocks (I1-L1-A1-C1, I2-...), never one big blended block.

## Step 1: Research before writing (mandatory)

1. Identify the mode: **Case report** (a court judgment) or **Incident report** (a real event that may or may not have reached court).
2. Search the web for the current position. Media law in Malaysia changes fast (e.g. CMA 1998 amendments, Online Safety Act 2025, Malaysian Media Council Act 2025, platform licensing). Check whether each law cited is **in force, amended, proposed, or repealed**, and flag that status in the report.
3. For a judgment: find the judgment or reliable reports of it (eJudgment / Kehakiman portal, CommonLII, law reports, Bar Council, reputable news). Note the court level, date, judges, and outcome. Note any appeal.
4. For an incident: gather facts from at least two reputable sources (mainstream news + NGO such as CIJ, SUARAM, Article 19, RSF, or official MCMC/police/AGC statements). Separate confirmed facts from allegations.
5. Never invent cases, sections, quotes, or references. If a citation cannot be verified, say "[citation to verify]" rather than guess. If sources conflict, say so plainly.

## Step 2: Report structure

### Mode A: Case report on a judgment

1. **Title** – case name and citation.
2. **Case details** – court, date, coram (judges), parties, procedural history (how it got to this court).
3. **Material facts** – only the facts that matter legally, short and neutral.
4. **ILAC per issue**
   - Issue: as framed by the court (and, if relevant, an issue the court missed).
   - Law: the provisions and precedents the court relied on.
   - Application: how the court reasoned (majority, then any dissent). Identify the *ratio decidendi* (the binding legal reason for the decision) and notable *obiter dicta* (remarks not essential to the decision).
   - Conclusion: the holding on that issue.
5. **Outcome / order**.
6. **Discussion** (see Step 3).
7. **Conclusion of the report** – 1 paragraph.
8. **References** (APA 7).

### Mode B: Incident / news event report

1. **Title** – descriptive, neutral (no loaded words).
2. **Background and timeline** – what happened, who, when; mark what is confirmed vs alleged.
3. **Stakeholders** – speaker/publisher, platform, regulator (MCMC, KDN, police, AGC), affected parties.
4. **ILAC per issue** – typical issues: Was an offence or breach likely committed? Was the state action lawful (power, procedure)? Is the restriction constitutional under Article 10? What civil liability exists (defamation)? What platform obligations apply?
   - Application must go element by element and argue both ways where the facts allow.
   - Conclusion states the likely position and why it is uncertain if it is (e.g. no charge yet, court not yet ruled).
5. **Current status** – charged / investigated / dropped / pending, with date checked.
6. **Discussion** (see Step 3).
7. **Conclusion of the report**.
8. **References** (APA 7).

## Writing each ILAC part well

### Issue
- One sentence, framed as a question, naming the law and the actor.
- Weak: "Freedom of speech."
- Strong: "Whether X's TikTok video could constitute an offence under section 233(1)(a) of the Communications and Multimedia Act 1998, and if so, whether prosecution is a permissible restriction under Article 10(2)(a) of the Federal Constitution."

### Law
- Start from the Constitution (Art. 10(1)(a), the permitted grounds in Art. 10(2)(a), Art. 10(4) where race/religion/royalty are involved, Art. 149/150 if relevant), then the statute, then cases.
- Quote or paraphrase each statutory provision accurately and break it into **elements** (e.g. conduct + character of content + mental element/intent).
- Explain every legal term in plain language the first time (e.g. "*mens rea* – the mental element, such as intention").
- Give case authority with what it decided, not just its name.
- Flag status: in force / amended (year) / repealed (year) / proposed.

### Application
This is where marks are earned. Rules:
- Take each element in turn and match it to specific facts.
- Use "On one hand... on the other hand..." to give the strongest argument for each side (prosecution/state vs defence/speaker).
- Address constitutionality where relevant: Is the restriction on a permitted Art. 10(2) ground? Is it proportionate? Note how Malaysian courts have approached this and where the case law is unsettled.
- Separate (a) what the law says, (b) how it is enforced in practice, (c) what is contested.
- Weak: "The video was offensive so section 233 applies."
- Strong: "The first element, use of network facilities, is clearly met since the video was uploaded to TikTok. The contested element is whether the content is 'offensive in character' [check current wording after amendment]. The state would argue... The speaker would argue... Because the provision does not define the term, outcome depends on judicial interpretation, which has been inconsistent."

### Conclusion (per issue)
- Directly answer the Issue question in 1–3 sentences.
- State certainty: "likely", "arguable", "unlikely", and why.
- No new law or facts here.

## Step 3: Discussion (theory + comparison + critique)

After the ILAC blocks, write a Discussion that lifts the analysis from law to media and communication studies. Use these four moves, in this order:

1. **Why the state regulates here** – name the justification (national security, public order, race and religion, morality, reputation, privacy, consumer protection, economic policy) and the regulator type (state command-and-control, self-regulation, co-regulation, platform governance, courts).
2. **Theoretical lens** – pick the 1–2 frameworks that fit best and say why; also say where a theory fits poorly. Options:
   - Normative press theories (authoritarian, libertarian, social responsibility, Soviet communist, development media) and their critiques
   - Hallin & Mancini media system models (and the problem of applying a Western-derived typology to Malaysia)
   - Habermas's public sphere; political economy of media; hegemony and state power
   - Development journalism and the "Asian values" debate, with critiques
   - Media convergence / law lagging behind digital media; platform governance
3. **Comparative analysis** – choose 2–3 jurisdictions that illuminate the issue and state why each was chosen. Present a table, then a short analysis:

   | Dimension | Malaysia | Comparator 1 | Comparator 2 | International standard (ICCPR Art. 19) |
   |---|---|---|---|---|
   | Legal basis | | | | |
   | Threshold for restriction | | | | |
   | Who decides / regulator | | | | |
   | Penalty / remedy | | | | |
   | Safeguards (judicial review, defences) | | | | |

   Good pairings: Singapore (POFMA, similar heritage), UK (Communications Act 2003 s.127, Online Safety Act 2023), India (Shreya Singhal striking down IT Act s.66A), US (First Amendment contrast), EU (ECHR Art. 10, DSA), Indonesia (ITE Law), Thailand (lèse-majesté / Computer Crime Act), Philippines (cyberlibel). Verify each comparator's current status before citing it.
4. **Competing arguments and significance** – give the government's justification and the civil society / scholarly critique at full strength, without picking a side. Close with what the case or incident shows about the trajectory of Malaysian media regulation (e.g. reform promises vs enforcement after changes of government).

Keep the Discussion analytical, not descriptive. Every paragraph should make a point, support it, and link it back to the ILAC findings.

## Style rules

- Neutral, scholarly tone, especially on race, religion, royalty, and politics. Use neutral verbs ("stated", "argued", "alleged"), not loaded ones.
- Lead each section with its point, then explain.
- Headings for each part; paragraphs, not bullet lists, inside Application and Discussion.
- Malaysian case citation format: *Case Name* [Year] Volume Report Page (Court), e.g. *Public Prosecutor v Azmi Sharom* [2015] 6 MLJ 751 (FC). Verify every citation.
- Statutes: full name, year, Act number where known, section, e.g. Communications and Multimedia Act 1998 (Act 588), s. 233.
- APA 7: legal materials follow the jurisdiction's legal citation style inside APA; in-text cite cases as *Case Name* (Year). Journal articles, books, NGO reports, and news use standard APA 7.
- Default length if not specified: case report ~1,800–2,500 words; incident report ~1,500–2,200 words. Discussion ≈ 35–40% of the total.
