# Public Prosecutor v Azmi Sharom [2015] 6 MLJ 751 (FC): Is the Sedition Act 1948 Constitutional?

*COM741 Media Law and Regulations. Case report.* ILAC is doctrinal: it says what the law is and how it applies ("law in the books"). It cannot show whom the law falls on or whether it is justified ("law in action"). The report therefore has two halves: ILAC per issue, then a Discussion.

## Case details

- **Court and date:** Federal Court, Putrajaya, Criminal Reference No. 06-5-12-2014(W), 6 October 2015; also [2015] 8 CLJ 921.
- **Coram:** Arifin Zakaria CJ, Raus Sharif PCA, Zulkefli Ahmad Makinudin CJ (Malaya), Abdull Hamid Embong FCJ, Suriyadi Halim Omar FCJ. Arifin Zakaria CJ delivered one judgment for the court; there was no dissent.
- **Parties:** Public Prosecutor (plaintiff); Azmi Sharom, law academic, University of Malaya (defendant).
- **Procedural history:** The defendant was charged in the Kuala Lumpur Sessions Court. On 5 November 2014 the High Court referred two constitutional questions to the Federal Court under s. 84 of the Courts of Judicature Act 1964.

## Material facts

The Malay Mail Online (14 August 2014) quoted the defendant on the Selangor Menteri Besar dispute: "You don't want a repeat of that, where a secret meeting took place" and "I think what happened in Perak was legally wrong. The best thing to do is do it as legally and transparently as possible." He was charged under s. 4(1)(b) (uttering seditious words) and alternatively s. 4(1)(c) (publishing a seditious publication) of the Sedition Act 1948 (Act 15). The court did not decide whether his words were seditious; the reference was abstract.

## ILAC

### Issue 1: Is a pre-independence Act valid despite Article 10(2)?

**Issue.** Whether the Sedition Act 1948, made by the Federal Legislative Council before independence, is valid and enforceable when Article 10(2) allows speech restrictions only by "Parliament ... by law".

**Law.** Article 10(2)(a) and (4) refer to Parliament. Article 4(1) voids post-independence laws that are inconsistent with the Constitution. Article 162(1) continues "existing laws" (Art. 160(2): laws in operation before Merdeka Day) until repealed, with modifications; Art. 162(6) lets courts adapt them. *Assa Singh v Mentri Besar, Johore* [1968] treated an inconsistent existing law as "eclipsed", not void. *Nordin Salleh* [1992] (SC) held a state law could not restrict association. Status: the Act remains in force.

**Application.** The defence argued from the text and *Nordin Salleh*: only Parliament may restrict speech. The court distinguished that case twice: it concerned a state law and a post-independence law. It then read "Parliament may by law" harmoniously with Art. 162, reasoning that the framers, drafting Art. 162 with Art. 10(2) in view, could have excluded the Act and did not. The 1946 Working Committee report on continuing existing laws supported this. An existing law is therefore void only if it cannot be modified to accord with the Constitution.

On one hand, the reasoning is coherent: independence required legal continuity. On the other, the text is strong, and continuity shows only that the Act survives, not that it satisfies the requirement of a law by Parliament. The court did not ask whether Parliament's later revision and amendments adopted the Act, an alternative route. The effect is to move the real contest to Issue 3.

**Conclusion.** Yes. The Act is valid and enforceable as an existing law saved by Art. 162. This is the court's clear holding.

### Issue 2: May courts require restrictions to be "reasonable"?

**Issue.** Whether courts may read "reasonable" into "restrictions" in Article 10(2), or whether reasonableness is for Parliament alone.

**Law.** Article 4(2)(b) bars challenge on the ground that Parliament did not deem a restriction necessary or expedient. *PP v Pung Chen Choon* [1994] (SC) presumed constitutionality and asked only whether a law falls within an Art. 10(2)(a) head by a "real and proximate" connection. *Dr Mohd Nasir Hashim* [2007] (CA) and *Sivarasa Rasiah* [2010] (FC) read in "reasonable".

**Application.** The plaintiff argued that *Sivarasa* overlooked *Pung Chen Choon* and that the framers deliberately removed "reasonable" from the draft. The court agreed, citing the drafting history. It rejected reasoning from *Ooi Ah Phua* [1975] about "as soon as may be" in Art. 5(3): that concerned timing, and inserting "reasonable" would be "rewriting" Art. 10(2). Reasonableness is therefore "strictly within the discretion of the Legislature".

The counter-argument, made by Bon and Yong (2017), is that omitting a word does not oust judicial assessment, because Arts. 8 and 10 interact. A further question is status. The statute was held proportionate in Issue 3, so the case would have ended the same way had *Sivarasa* stood. The departure from *Sivarasa* on reasonableness is thus arguably obiter, though it signals the court's deferential stance.

**Conclusion.** No. The court held that reasonableness is for Parliament, departing from *Sivarasa*. Whether this is ratio or obiter is arguable.

### Issue 3: Is section 4(1) within Article 10(2)(a) and proportionate?

**Issue.** Whether s. 4(1) of the Sedition Act, which penalises acts, words and publications with a "seditious tendency", falls within Article 10(2)(a) and passes the proportionality test.

**Law.** Section 3(1)(a)-(f) defines "seditious tendency": hatred, contempt or disaffection against the Government, ill will between races, and questioning matters protected by Part III or Arts. 152, 153 and 181. Section 3(2) exempts, for example, pointing out errors in a Government. Article 10(4) permits laws against questioning those matters. Proportionality (*Nyambirai*; *de Freitas*) asks whether the objective is important enough, the measure rationally connected, and the means no more than necessary; the court traced it to Art. 8(1).

**Application.** The court said s. 4(1) targets tendencies in s. 3(1)(a)-(f), so it is not "too remote" from the Art. 10(2)(a) heads, and it is not a "total prohibition" because of the s. 3(2) exceptions. For the state, limbs (e) and (f) fit closely: race relations and Art. 10(4). For the defence, limb (a), "disaffection" against any Government, is hard to link to the security of the Federation, and the court did not examine the limbs separately.

The proportionality analysis is also thin. The court recited the three-part test but did not apply importance, rational connection and minimal impairment in turn. It did not consider whether narrower offences, such as incitement to violence, would serve the aim, or s. 3(3), under which intention is irrelevant. Later, in *Mat Shuhaimi bin Shafiei v Kerajaan Malaysia* [2017] 1 MLRA 190 the Court of Appeal declared s. 3(3) contrary to Art. 10. The Federal Court set that declaration aside on 8 January 2018 as an abuse of process, without deciding the merits ([2018] MYFC 2, from a secondary summary [to verify]). Section 3(3) therefore remains open.

**Conclusion.** Yes. Section 4(1) was held within Art. 10(2)(a) and proportionate. The holding binds, but the thin analysis makes future challenges, especially to s. 3(3), arguable.

## Outcome

The first question was answered in the negative (s. 4(1) does not contravene Art. 10(2)) and the second in the positive (the Act is valid). The case was remitted to the Sessions Court. The Attorney General withdrew the charge on 12 February 2016 under Art. 145(3).

## Discussion

### Why the state regulates, and how

The justifications are security and public order, race relations, and protection of Rulers and Government (Art. 10(2)(a), Art. 10(4), s. 3(1)). The regulator is state command-and-control: criminal prosecution, filtered by the Attorney General's discretion under Art. 145(3). In law in action, the Act's reach grew. The ICJ recorded a sharp rise in sedition cases from 2014, affecting academics, lawyers, politicians, students and activists (Columbia Global Freedom of Expression, n.d.). This links to Issue 3. The s. 3(2) exceptions arguably covered Azmi's remarks, which criticised a constitutional process, yet he was charged; a test applied only at the level of the text could not see that gap. His charge ended through unexplained discretion, not a legal limit.

### Theoretical lenses

Authoritarian theory (Siebert et al., 1956), under which speech is subordinate to state order, fits partly. Section 3(1)(a) makes "disaffection" against Government an offence, and the court's deference mirrors the theory. It fits poorly because Malaysia has contested elections, independent courts and a constitutional speech guarantee, and the theory addresses the press, not an academic's comment.

Habermas's public sphere (1989) fits better. Azmi's remarks were rational-critical commentary on a constitutional process, the discourse the theory protects. It too fits imperfectly: it assumes equal participants and says little about the ethnic-plurality rationale invoked in Malaysia.

### Comparison

I chose Singapore (it also had a "Sedition Act 1948" and shares the colonial origin), India (its Art. 19(2) contains the "reasonable" standard Malaysia rejected) and the UK (the source of the tendency offence).

Of the comparators, Singapore and the UK abolished sedition; India narrowed it by reading down, then replaced it. Malaysia alone keeps the tendency test with intention irrelevant, protected by judicial deference rather than reading down. India's courts apply "reasonable" textually, while Malaysia's court removed it and kept proportionality, so much turns on one adjective. Singapore's route is instructive: it repealed the Act but kept a narrowed offence requiring intent, so abolition did not mean the absence of regulation.

### Competing arguments and significance

The government's case, accepted in the judgment, is that Art. 10(4) lets Parliament protect entrenched matters and s. 3(2) preserves legitimate criticism. Critics answer that the Act is broad, ignores intention and is applied selectively (ICJ, as reported by Columbia Global Freedom of Expression, n.d.). The ICCPR benchmark is soft because Malaysia is not a party.

Evaluation of the judgment. The ruling on existing law persuades on continuity but leaves the word "Parliament" unanswered. Replacing reasonableness with proportionality is a compromise whose force depends on application, and here little was shown. The deference sits uneasily with *Sivarasa*, and with the Court of Appeal's later willingness in *Mat Shuhaimi* to test s. 3(3).

The decision closed the constitutional route against s. 4(1) and left reform to Parliament. On 29 August 2026 the Prime Minister called for colonial-era laws to be abolished and DAP's Ramkarpal Singh urged repeal (Al As, 2026); I found no repeal bill. The trajectory shows reform promises still outpacing practice.

## Conclusion of the report

The Federal Court held that the Sedition Act 1948 survives through Art. 162 and that s. 4(1) satisfies Art. 10(2)(a) and proportionality. Its reasoning on existing law is clear; its reasoning on proportionality is thin and leaves s. 3(3) unresolved. The Act is in force as at 24 October 2026, and the real debate is now legislative.
