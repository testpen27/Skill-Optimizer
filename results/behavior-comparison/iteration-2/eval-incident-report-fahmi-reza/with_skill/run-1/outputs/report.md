# Incident Report: The Prosecution of Fahmi Reza under Section 233 of the Communications and Multimedia Act 1998 for a Clown Caricature of Najib Razak (2016)

*COM741 Media Law and Regulations. Position checked 23 October 2026.*

**Why two halves.** ILAC (Issue, Law, Application, Conclusion) is a doctrinal method: it says what the law is and how it applies ("law in the books"). It cannot show who the law falls on or whether it is justified ("law in action"). Part 1 applies ILAC to three issues; Part 2 is a theory-driven comparative Discussion.

## 2. ILAC analysis

### Issue 1: Did the Facebook post satisfy the elements of s. 233(1)(a) CMA as it stood in 2016?

**Law.** Communications and Multimedia Act 1998 (Act 588), s. 233(1)(a) (wording before 11 February 2025) criminalised using network facilities to make and initiate transmission of a communication that is "obscene, indecent, false, menacing or offensive in character" *with intent to annoy, abuse, threaten or harass another person* (wording from memory; [to verify against the Gazette]). Four elements follow: (i) use of network facilities or services; (ii) a communication initiated by the accused; (iii) content of a prohibited character; (iv) *mens rea* (the mental element), namely intent to annoy, abuse, threaten or harass. The penalty was a fine up to RM50,000, one year's imprisonment, or both (Malay Mail, 2018a). Status: this wording was replaced in part in 2025 (see section 3). The Federal Court later read the offence as requiring an *objective* test for the character of the content and intent that is the "dominant purpose" and "cannot be inferred from the content alone" (*Government of Malaysia v Heidy Quah Gaik Li* [2026] MLJU 384 (FC), as summarised by Lee Hishammuddin Allen & Gledhill, 2026).

**Application.** Elements (i) and (ii) are not contestable: Fahmi posted the image himself on Facebook. The dispute lies in (iii) and (iv). On character, the prosecution argued, and two courts accepted, that depicting a sitting Prime Minister as a clown is "offensive" (Malay Mail, 2018a, 2019). The defence's strongest reply is that the word "offensive" is undefined, that political satire of a public figure is not offensive "in character" in the way obscene or menacing material is, and that the image drew on the Sedition Act caption and the 1MDB allegations, so it was commentary rather than abuse. On intent, the prosecution would infer intent to annoy from the image itself and its mocking message; the defence would say that Fahmi's purpose was to protest corruption and to provoke debate, so annoyance was at most a side effect. The Sessions Court found that the defence had not raised a reasonable doubt (Malaysiakini, 2018). The High Court said it relied on the evidence of witnesses, including the complainants, and maintained the conviction (Malay Mail, 2018a). Those reports do not set out the reasoning on intent in detail [grounds of judgment to verify]. The point of difference from the 2026 position is decisive: under *Heidy Quah*, intent cannot be inferred from the content alone. The convictions in 2018 and 2019 appear to have rested heavily on the content of the image, so they are exposed to criticism under the later test, although the later test does not itself reopen them.

**Conclusion.** On the law and the courts' findings as they stood, an offence was made out and the conviction was lawful, being upheld on appeal. It is arguable, and in my view probable, that the same facts would not now support a charge, because the post was political satire and the intent to annoy was not shown independently of its content.

### Issue 2: Is a prosecution under s. 233(1)(a) for political satire a permissible restriction under Article 10(2)(a) of the Federal Constitution?

**Law.** Article 10(1)(a) guarantees freedom of speech and expression. Article 10(2)(a) allows Parliament to restrict it by law as it "deems necessary or expedient" in the interest of security, friendly relations, public order or morality, or to protect parliamentary privilege or provide against contempt of court, defamation or incitement to an offence. Article 4(1) makes the Constitution the supreme law. Malaysian courts have been deferential on the "necessary or expedient" standard: the Federal Court upheld the Sedition Act against an Article 10 challenge in *Public Prosecutor v Azmi Sharom* [2015] 6 MLJ 751 (FC). The key decisions on s. 233 are *Heidy Quah Gaik Li v Government of Malaysia* (CoA, 19 August 2025), which struck out "offensive" and "annoy" as inconsistent with Article 10 (Malay Mail, 2025), and the Federal Court's reversal on 6 February 2026, which upheld both words (Malay Mail, 2026; Lee Hishammuddin Allen & Gledhill, 2026). Neither decision was about Fahmi Reza; both concerned the pre-2025 wording.

**Application.** The state's case is that s. 233(1)(a) is a law restricting speech on a permitted ground, in particular public order and morality, and that it covers only communications outside the protection of Article 10: indecent or obscene material, threats, knowingly false or malicious content, and attacks on race, religion or the monarchy (Lee Hishammuddin Allen & Gledhill, 2026). The Federal Court accepted this and used the doctrine of avoiding vagueness to "read down" the provision, focusing on intent as the constitutional core. The speaker's case, which the Court of Appeal accepted in 2025, is that "annoy" and "offensive" are not on the Article 10(2)(a) list: annoyance is not public order, and a vague standard lets enforcement turn on who is annoyed. Applied to Fahmi, the contest is sharp. A caricature of a head of government on a corruption allegation is core political speech, which even the Federal Court treated as protected (Malay Mail, 2026, on Heidy Quah's post). The reading-down therefore helps him on the facts, but it does not answer the objection that the statutory wording, on its face, penalises mere annoyance. Two points remain uncertain: the Federal Court's treatment of Article 4(2) and its proportionality analysis, which I could not read in full [judgment to verify]. Fahmi's own constitutional challenge was never decided on the merits, since the Federal Court set it aside for procedural non-compliance (Free Malaysia Today, 2016; Malaysiakini, 2018).

**Conclusion.** Section 233(1)(a) is now constitutionally valid at the highest level, but only as read down to require demonstrable dominant intent; whether the original prosecution of satire met that standard is doubtful. The answer is certain as to the provision's validity and uncertain as to Fahmi's own case, as the merits of his challenge were never decided.

### Issue 3: Was the manner of prosecution lawful, given two charges over similar images and the later withdrawal?

**Law.** The Public Prosecutor holds the power to institute, conduct or discontinue criminal proceedings under Article 145(3) of the Federal Constitution; that power is subject to judicial review only in narrow cases of bad faith or improper purpose [authority to verify]. A withdrawal before the accused is called on to plead or after defence may lead to a discharge not amounting to an acquittal or a full acquittal (Criminal Procedure Code, s. 254) [to verify].

**Application.** The prosecution of the same artist in two courts over the same image meant two exposures to jeopardy. The Kuala Lumpur charge was withdrawn after representations by his counsel and the discharge followed on 11 October 2018 (Malay Mail, 2018b), after the change of government in May 2018. The Ipoh case continued and ended in conviction. The High Court expressly reduced the sentence on the strength of the ministry's announced intention to amend s. 233(1)(a) and of the Kuala Lumpur discharge (Malay Mail, 2018a), and the prosecution later dropped its appeal for a heavier sentence (Malay Mail, 2019). On one hand, this shows the discretion operating normally: each charge was tried on its own evidence, in the territory where the content was viewed. On the other hand, a conviction in one court and a withdrawal in the other for the same image looks arbitrary, and the timing tracks the political context. No court has found bad faith, and nothing I found shows the prosecution was selective by law.

**Conclusion.** The prosecution was procedurally lawful; its consistency and motive are open to criticism, but no court has found abuse of process.

## 3. What has happened since, and how s. 233 has changed

- **Fahmi's conviction.** It stands: the Court of Appeal dismissed his appeal on 5 July 2019. Whether he sought leave to the Federal Court is not confirmed [to verify].
- **Repeat enforcement.** Fahmi was arrested in April 2021 over a Spotify playlist (Sedition Act s. 4(1) and CMA s. 233); in December 2024 over a Sabah poster of Musa Aman (Sedition Act, CMA s. 233 and Penal Code s. 504), with an entry ban reported in May 2025 (Wikipedia, 2026; The Sun, 2024; Malay Mail, 2025b); and on 19 December 2025 over posts about Tunku Ismail of Johor under the Sedition Act and s. 233, released on police bail, phone retained (Malay Mail, 2025c; NST, 2025). No outcome of the 2024 and 2025 investigations was found.
- **Legislative change.** The Communications and Multimedia (Amendment) Act 2025 came into force on 11 February 2025 (except ss. 92 and 112). It replaced "offensive" with "grossly offensive" in s. 233(1)(a), kept "annoy", added a fraud and dishonesty limb, and inserted Explanations 1 to 6 on prohibited content, reportedly including a good-faith exception for true statements and opinion (Conventus Law, 2025; Rajah & Tann Asia, 2025) [wording to verify]. The maximum penalty rose from RM50,000 and one year to RM500,000 and two years, with a daily fine of RM5,000 (was RM1,000), and five years where the victim is a minor (Conventus Law, 2025). The amended text has not yet been tested in court, and the Fahmi-era wording survives for older offences.
- **Case law.** Court of Appeal, 19 August 2025: words struck down, prospective effect (Malay Mail, 2025). Federal Court, 6 February 2026: reinstated, read down, and Heidy Quah's prosecution held unjustified (Malay Mail, 2026).
- **Related laws in force.** The Online Safety Act 2025 (in force 1 January 2026) and the Malaysian Media Council Act 2025 (in force 14 June 2025) are in the status snapshot; the Sedition Act 1948 remains in force.

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1 Why the state regulates here

The justifications offered are public order, protection of reputation and dignity, and, since 2025, online harm and fraud. The regulator is a state command-and-control model: MCMC investigates and prosecutes, police run parallel sedition inquiries, and courts supervise. The Fahmi case illustrates that a provision justified as protection against online abuse was used against a political cartoon, and that the same provision is enforced by agencies with stakes in the target.

### 4.2 Theoretical lenses

*Habermas's public sphere* fits well. Satire is a way for citizens to take part in rational-critical debate about a leader's conduct, and s. 233 prosecution raises the cost of that participation, narrowing the sphere. It fits poorly because Habermas assumes an ideal of reasoned argument, whereas a clown image is mockery; Malaysian courts treated that mockery as the harm.

*Normative press theory* gives a second lens. Siebert, Peterson and Schramm's authoritarian theory describes licensing and punishment of criticism of rulers; libertarian theory describes the market of ideas. Neither fits cleanly. Malaysia had a formally independent judiciary, appeals, a change of government and eventually a Federal Court that found a prosecution unjustified. The case is better described as a hybrid in which law is used selectively and unevenly, and the typology has limited use in a mixed system.

*Law lagging convergence* is a third point. Section 233 was enacted in 1998 for telephone-style "network facilities" and applies unchanged to Facebook posts and memes, with "annoy" carried over from a harassment model [origin to verify].

### 4.3 Comparative analysis

Comparators were chosen because India struck down a nearly identical provision, and the United Kingdom is the source of the "grossly offensive" formula that Malaysia adopted in 2025.

| Dimension | Malaysia | India | United Kingdom | ICCPR Art. 19 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Legal basis | CMA 1998 s. 233(1)(a), as amended 2025 | IT Act 2000 s. 66A (struck down) | Communications Act 2003 s. 127; Online Safety Act 2023 offences | ICCPR Art. 19(3) |
| Threshold | "Grossly offensive" (2025); intent to annoy, read down to dominant intent (2026) | Struck down for vagueness: "grossly offensive", "annoyance", "inconvenience" (*Shreya Singhal v Union of India* (2015) 5 SCC 1) | Must be more than offensive or shocking (*DPP v Chambers* [2012] EWHC 2157 (Admin)) | Legality, legitimate aim, necessity and proportionality [verify General Comment 34 paragraph on public figures] |
| Who decides | MCMC prosecutors, Public Prosecutor; courts | Courts | Crown Prosecution Service, courts | Independent judiciary |
| Penalty | Up to RM500,000 and 2 years | Was 3 years | Fine or up to 6 months under s. 127 [verify] | Must be proportionate |
| Safeguards | Reading down; good-faith explanation (reported) | Provision void | Context and satire considered | Public figures should tolerate more criticism |

India shows that the most direct remedy, striking down the language, was rejected by Malaysia's Federal Court in favour of a narrowing interpretation, which leaves the text in place but depends on prosecutors to apply it. The United Kingdom shows that courts can protect a joke or satire by construing "grossly offensive" strictly, which is the route open to Malaysian courts after 2025; Malaysia's penalty, however, is much higher. Both comparisons are from memory and should be checked [to verify each comparator's current status, including the 2023 UK reform].

### 4.4 Competing arguments and significance

The government's position is that the Federal Court has confirmed the law's validity, that the 2025 amendment raised the threshold and added protections, and that online abuse and fraud need effective penalties. Cabinet said it accepts the judgment (Malay Mail, 2026). The civil society view, put by ARTICLE 19, is that the provision still fails legality, necessity and proportionality, that "annoy" and "grossly offensive" remain vague, and that the Federal Court's decision is a setback for expression, though it welcomed the finding that political views are protected (ARTICLE 19, 2026). The record of Fahmi himself supports the critics' practical point: he was prosecuted by a government he satirised, discharged in one court after the government changed, and arrested again under the same section in 2021, 2024 and 2025. It supports the state's point that the courts, in 2018 (sentence), 2019 (prosecution dropped its appeal) and 2026, have moved towards restraint. The trajectory is therefore mixed: reform promises in 2018 produced a 2025 amendment that raised the threshold word but multiplied the penalty tenfold, and the law's day-to-day effect depends on prosecutorial discretion rather than on a settled constitutional limit.

## 5. Conclusion of the report

The 2018 conviction was lawful on the law as then applied, but it rested on content-based inference of intent that the Federal Court has since rejected, and on a provision whose "offensive" and "annoy" wording the courts now accept only as read down. The same facts would probably not support a charge today, though the 2025 text is untested and enforcement against Fahmi has continued. Whether the prosecution was constitutional was never decided in his own case; the better view, after *Heidy Quah*, is that the provision survives but his prosecution would sit uneasily within it.
