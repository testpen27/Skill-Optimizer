# *Public Prosecutor v Azmi Sharom* [2015] 6 MLJ 751 (FC): Is the Sedition Act 1948 Constitutional?

This report uses ILAC (Issue, Law, Application, Conclusion), a doctrinal method that shows what the law is and how it applies (law in the books). ILAC cannot show who the law falls on or whether it is justified (law in action), so a Discussion follows the ILAC blocks.

## Case details

- **Citation:** *Public Prosecutor v Azmi Sharom* [2015] 6 MLJ 751; [2015] 6 AMR 338; [2015] 8 CLJ 921 (FC), Criminal Reference No. 06-5-12-2014(W).
- **Date and coram:** 6 October 2015. Arifin Zakaria CJ, Raus Sharif PCA, Zulkefli Ahmad Makinudin CJ (Malaya), Abdull Hamid Embong and Suriyadi Halim Omar FCJJ. Arifin Zakaria CJ delivered the court's judgment.
- **Procedural history:** The accused was charged in the Sessions Court and challenged the Sedition Act 1948 (Act 15). Two questions were referred to the Federal Court under s. 84 of the Courts of Judicature Act 1964: (a) whether s. 4(1) contravenes Article 10(2) of the Federal Constitution and is void under Article 4(1); (b) whether the Act is valid and enforceable (Bon & Yong, 2017).

## Material facts

Azmi Sharom, an Associate Professor of Law at Universiti Malaya, was quoted in a Malay Mail Online article of 14 August 2014 on a Selangor political impasse. He said that what happened in Perak in 2009 was "legally wrong" and that a vote should be held in the legislative assembly. He was charged on 2 September 2014 under s. 4(1)(b) (uttering seditious words) and alternatively s. 4(1)(c) (publishing a seditious publication). A first offence carried up to three years' imprisonment, a RM5,000 fine, or both (Columbia GFoE, 2016). The defence argued that the Act was made by the colonial Federal Legislative Council, not "Parliament", so it could not restrict speech under Article 10(2).

## Issue 1: Is the Act valid when Parliament did not make it?

**Issue.** Whether a pre-Merdeka statute made by the Federal Legislative Council can restrict speech, when Article 10(2) says "Parliament may by law" impose restrictions.

**Law.** Article 10(1)(a) guarantees citizens freedom of speech. Article 10(2)(a) lets Parliament impose restrictions it "deems necessary or expedient" for security, public order and other listed grounds. Article 162 continues existing laws, subject to modifications that bring them into conformity with the Constitution. The defence relied on *Nordin Salleh v Kerajaan Negeri Kelantan* [1993] 2 AMR 3086 (SC), which treated Article 10(2) restrictions as Parliament's to make. Status: the Act is in force (checked 23 October 2026).

**Application.** On one hand, *Nordin Salleh* read Article 10(2) as reserving restriction to Parliament, and a 1948 ordinance was never passed by it. On the other hand, the court held that "Parliament may by law" must not be read in a "highly restrictive and rigid" way and must be read harmoniously with Article 162. The reasoning is structural. Article 162 exists to keep existing law working from Merdeka Day, and the court inferred that the framers intended existing law to "continue to be valid and enforceable". If Article 10(2) invalidated every pre-1957 restriction, Article 162 would do little. An existing law falls only if it cannot be modified to conform. As I read it, *Nordin Salleh* is distinguishable because it concerned a state constitutional provision, not an existing law saved by Article 162. The weakness is that the court did not say how far modification may go.

**Conclusion.** The Act is valid and enforceable. This is a firm conclusion because it rests on the text of Article 162.

## Issue 2: What standard governs restrictions under Article 10(2)?

**Issue.** Whether a restriction under Article 10(2)(a) must be "reasonable", and if not, what limits Parliament's power.

**Law.** In *Sivarasa Rasiah v Badan Peguam Malaysia* [2010] 2 AMR 301 (FC), the court read "reasonable" into Article 10(2) and required proportionality. Shortly before *Azmi Sharom*, Raus Sharif PCA in *PP v Yuneswaran Ramaraj* [2015] 6 AMR 271 (CA) called that reading *obiter dicta* (a remark that does not bind) and a usurpation of Parliament's role, preferring *PP v Pung Chen Choon* [1994] 1 AMR 689 (SC), under which Parliament judges what is reasonable. Proportionality has three limbs: the objective must justify limiting the right; the measure must be rationally connected to it; and the means must impair the right no more than necessary.

**Application.** The court took a middle course. It sided with *Pung Chen Choon* and *Yuneswaran*: courts do not decide whether Parliament's restriction is reasonable. Yet it agreed with *Sivarasa* that the power is not unlimited and that restrictions "must pass the proportionality test". The ratio *decidendi* (the rule the decision rests on) is therefore that courts do not read "reasonable" into Article 10(2), but every restriction must be proportionate. The halves sit uneasily. Bon and Yong (2017) argue that proportionality review cannot avoid asking whether a restriction is reasonable, since both draw on Article 8 (equality), so the change may be more label than substance. It still matters in practice: "proportionate" invites scrutiny of the means, while "reasonable" invites scrutiny of the policy.

**Conclusion.** Restrictions need not be "reasonable" in the *Sivarasa* sense but must be proportionate. This is the more durable part of the ratio.

## Issue 3: Does s. 4(1) pass the proportionality test?

**Issue.** Whether s. 4(1), which criminalises seditious words and publications, is a proportionate restriction under Article 10(2)(a).

**Law.** Section 3(1) defines "seditious tendency" to include bringing the Ruler or Government into hatred or contempt, promoting ill-will between races, and questioning matters entrenched in Part III or Articles 152, 153 and 181. Section 3(2) excepts, for example, pointing out errors or defects in the Government or Constitution, or seeking lawful change; the exceptions do not cover s. 3(1)(f). Section 3(3) makes the speaker's intention irrelevant. The Sedition (Amendment) Act 2015 (A1485) later changed the Act; sources conflict on commencement and on s. 3(1)(a) [to verify].

**Application.** The state would say that protecting public order is a legitimate objective, that criminalising seditious speech is rationally connected to it, and that s. 3(2) leaves room for criticism. The court agreed: because of s. 3(2), s. 4(1) is not a "total prohibition" of speech, so it is not disproportionate. The defence has three counterpoints. First, the third limb asks whether the means go no further than necessary, yet s. 3(3) removes intention, so a sincere critic and a hate-monger are equally liable. Second, the exceptions exclude s. 3(1)(f), the most politically sensitive limb. Third, the case itself shows the gap. Azmi Sharom's remarks were legal commentary, arguably within the s. 3(2) exception, yet he was prosecuted until the Attorney General withdrew the charges on 12 February 2016 under Article 145(3). The safeguards therefore operate through prosecutorial discretion, not by preventing a charge. The court also did not test whether a narrower offence, requiring intent or incitement to violence, would serve the same aim. Section 3(3) was not before it. In *Mat Shuhaimi Shafiei v Kerajaan Malaysia* (CA, 25 November 2016) the Court of Appeal struck down s. 3(3) for unequal treatment against Penal Code s. 505, which requires intent. The Federal Court reversed in January 2018, but on abuse of process and *res judicata* (a matter already decided), not the merits, so the question remains open.

**Conclusion.** Section 4(1) is constitutional: it "does not run counter to Article 10(2)(a)". The reasoning is open to challenge on the third limb, because it relied on the exceptions without testing intention or narrower alternatives.

## Outcome

The Federal Court answered the referred questions against the accused. The case returned to the Sessions Court (ARTICLE 19, 2015). A High Court transfer application failed on 13 January 2016, and the charges were withdrawn on 12 February 2016. ## Discussion

**Why the state regulates here.** The justifications are public order, security and inter-ethnic harmony. The Act dates from 1948 and the anti-communist emergency, and the post-1969 constitutional amendments widened Article 10(4) (Wikipedia, 2026). In 2015 the Home Minister defended amendments as preventing online racial disharmony (New Straits Times, 2015). The regulator is the state through criminal law, with the Attorney General controlling prosecution and courts deferring.

**Theoretical lens.** Siebert et al. (1956) give a partial fit. The authoritarian model, where speech is tolerated unless it threatens the state, matches the Act's protection of the government's standing ("hatred or contempt"), but fits poorly with the court's reasoned proportionality review and Malaysia's competitive elections. Development media theory fits the harmony rationale but not this speaker, a constitutional-law academic. Habermas's public sphere fits better: a lecturer offering a reasoned view on a constitutional crisis is the deliberation a public sphere needs, yet it was treated as a potential offence. Its limit is that Habermas assumes a settled liberal setting.

**Comparative analysis.** Singapore shares the colonial statute, the UK originated the offence, and India has an Article 19-style speech guarantee.

Malaysia keeps what the comparators have narrowed or dropped. Singapore replaced sedition with targeted offences (Ministry of Home Affairs, 2021), which answers the claim that repeal leaves a gap. India shows a court reading an offence down to incitement of violence, a step the Federal Court did not take. The UK shows sedition is dispensable in a mature democracy. One caution: India's successor provision is itself challenged as sedition renamed, so repeal alone does not guarantee a narrower law.

**Competing arguments.** The government's case is that the Act protects fragile racial and religious relations, that Article 10(4) contemplates it, and that Parliament, not judges, assesses security. ARTICLE 19 (2015) answers that the Act is vague, serves no legitimate aim beyond silencing dissent, and that "necessary or expedient" is a lower standard than international "necessity". It reports at least 78 people investigated or charged between January 2014 and April 2015.

**Significance.** *Azmi Sharom* secured proportionality as an Article 10 test but applied it deferentially. Reform has stalled: the Act remains in force, and in August 2026 an MP again urged repeal, noting Pakatan Harapan's manifesto commitments (The Vibes, 2026). The judicial route is narrow, since the Federal Court modifies existing laws only on individual applications. The Federal Court's February 2026 ruling upholding Communications and Multimedia Act 1998 s. 233(1)(a) [to verify] suggests deference continues.

## Conclusion of the report

The Federal Court held that the Sedition Act is valid under Article 162 and that s. 4(1) is a proportionate restriction under Article 10(2)(a). The ratio is that "Parliament" in Article 10(2) is read with Article 162, and that restrictions must be proportionate without being tested for reasonableness. The weakest point is the proportionality analysis, which relied on the s. 3(2) exceptions without testing intention or narrower alternatives. In practice the law bears harder on speakers than the doctrine suggests, and Malaysia is now unusual among its comparators.
