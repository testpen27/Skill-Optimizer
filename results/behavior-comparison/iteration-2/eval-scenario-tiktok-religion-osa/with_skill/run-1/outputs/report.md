# A Viral Video Mocking a Religious Group: The Legal Position of the Influencer and of TikTok in Malaysia

*Law checked 23 October 2026. [to verify] marks points not confirmed from a primary source.*

## 1. Scenario and method

This is a lecturer's hypothetical, so all facts are assumed. A Kuala Lumpur influencer posts a TikTok video mocking a religious group's practices. It goes viral. Police investigate under Penal Code s. 298A and the Communications and Multimedia Act 1998 (CMA) s. 233, and the Malaysian Communications and Multimedia Commission (MCMC) directs TikTok to remove it. No charge is assumed. The faiths of the influencer and the group are not stated, and some answers turn on them.

ILAC (Issue, Law, Application, Conclusion) is doctrinal: it shows what the law says ("law in the books"). The Discussion asks how it works in practice ("law in action"), hence two halves.

## 2. ILAC analysis

### Issue 1: Could the video be an offence under Penal Code s. 298A?

**Law.** Section 298A(1) punishes causing, attempting to cause, or being likely to cause, disharmony, enmity or ill-will on religious grounds between persons professing the same or different religions (paraphrase; wording and penalty [to verify]). The elements are a communication and a likely effect on religious harmony; no mental element (*mens rea*, such as intention) is stated. In *Mamat bin Daud v Government of Malaysia* [1988] 1 MLJ 119 (SC) [citation to verify], the majority held the section concerned Islamic offences, a State matter, so Parliament could not enact it outside the Federal Territories. The Court of Appeal in the *Alvivi* case (2014) [citation to verify] reportedly held that s. 298A(1) does not apply to non-Muslims.

**Application.** The communication element is met by the upload. The effect element is contested. The state would say "likely to cause" is a low threshold and that virality and complaints show risk. The influencer would answer that mocking practices is not inciting hatred of people, and that the absence of a fault element supports a narrow reading. Kuala Lumpur is a Federal Territory, so *Mamat Daud* does not remove Parliament's power here. Scope is harder. If the influencer is non-Muslim and the group Muslim, *Alvivi*, which also concerned ridicule of a Muslim practice, supports the defence. If the group is non-Muslim, *Alvivi* may be distinguished, since its reasoning turned on the section's Islamic subject-matter. I could not read the judgment, so its reach is unverified.

**Conclusion.** Liability is arguable but the weaker route, because scope depends on the parties' faiths.

### Issue 2: Could the video be an offence under CMA s. 233(1)(a), consistently with Article 10?

**Law.** Section 233(1)(a) punishes knowingly making and initiating transmission of content that is obscene, indecent, false, menacing or "grossly offensive" with intent to annoy, abuse, threaten or harass another person. The 2025 amendment (in force 11 February 2025) replaced "offensive" with "grossly offensive" and raised the penalty to RM500,000 or two years' imprisonment (reported). Article 10(2)(a) of the Federal Constitution permits restrictions on speech that are necessary or expedient for grounds including public order. *Government of Malaysia v Heidy Quah Gaik Li* (FC, 6 February 2026) [citation to verify] upheld the old wording; reports say intent was absent on its facts. The amended text is untested.

**Application.** Use of network facilities is met. "Grossly offensive" is contested. On one hand, the state would cite ridicule of sacred practice and say the higher 2025 threshold exists for such content. On the other hand, the influencer would call it satire protected by Article 10(1)(a), and the amendment reportedly adds an explanation protecting good-faith opinion [to verify], which helps if the video reads as opinion rather than abuse. Intent is decisive. The video addressed the public, so the prosecution must prove intent to annoy or harass another person, and *Heidy Quah* reportedly cautions against inferring intent from offensiveness alone [to verify]. The Evidence Act 1950 s. 114A presumption of publication helps on authorship, not intent. Viral religious mockery plausibly engages public order, but proportionality as applied is open.

**Conclusion.** Section 233 is the stronger route, but intent makes conviction uncertain.

### Issue 3: Must TikTok comply with the MCMC direction, and is it liable itself?

**Law.** TikTok reportedly holds an applications service provider licence under the CMA (early 2025). Licensees must assist authorities (CMA s. 263 [to verify]). The Online Safety Act 2025 (Act 866), in force 1 January 2026, binds licensees, including platforms with 8 million or more Malaysian users, through MCMC codes; high-priority content must be actioned within 24 hours, and code breaches may attract fines up to RM10 million (reported). Whether its nine harmful-content categories cover religious mockery is unverified.

**Application.** On one hand, TikTok is a licensee facing regulatory sanctions, so compliance is realistic; Malaysia made 1,862 removal requests to TikTok in the second half of 2023 (reported). On the other hand, if the direction rests on an unproven offence, judicial review is possible in principle, though unlikely commercially. Criminal liability is improbable because s. 233 requires that the person "knowingly" makes and initiates the content, which a passive host does not. The s. 114A presumption could be argued against a "host" but is rebuttable. Exposure arises if TikTok fails to act after notice.

**Conclusion.** TikTok almost certainly must comply and is unlikely to be criminally liable; its risk is regulatory.

## 3. Discussion

**Why the state regulates.** The justifications are public order and religious harmony in a plural society. The regulator type is command-and-control: police pursue the speaker, and MCMC directs the intermediary.

**Theoretical lens: platform governance.** I use one framework: platform governance, meaning governance of platforms by states and governance by platforms of users (Gorwa, 2019), with responsibility shared among states, platforms and users (Helberger et al., 2018). It fits because the scenario has three layers: criminal law targets the speaker, MCMC governs through TikTok, and TikTok governs the speaker through moderation (Gillespie, 2018). It explains the ILAC finding that intent and scope are contested while removal needs no finding of guilt, so the video can disappear before any court asks whether it was "grossly offensive". It fits less well because it assumes platforms negotiate their duties; licensing makes the Malaysian relationship hierarchical, and the framework says little about religion's constitutional position.

**Comparison.** Singapore is chosen for shared heritage, the same religious-harmony concern and a new platform regulator; the United Kingdom as a liberal contrast with a high threshold.

| Dimension | Malaysia | Singapore | UK | ICCPR Arts 19, 20(2) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Legal basis | Penal Code s. 298A; CMA s. 233; Act 866 | Penal Code s. 298A [to verify]; Online Safety (Relief and Accountability) Act 2025 | Public Order Act 1986 Part 3A; Online Safety Act 2023 [to verify] | Art. 19(3); Art. 20(2) |
| Threshold | Likely disharmony; grossly offensive with intent | Incitement of enmity is a harm category (reported) | Threatening, with intent to stir up hatred [to verify] | Necessity; incitement to hostility |
| Decider | Police, AGC, MCMC | Online Safety Commission (from 29 June 2026) | Courts, Ofcom [to verify] | Independent oversight preferred |
| Safeguards | Judicial review; intent | Appeal routes [to verify] | Free-speech protections [to verify] | Rabat Plan six-part test |

Malaysia's thresholds are lower than Article 20(2), which requires incitement to hostility, and than the UK's, which requires threatening conduct and intent. Malaysia and Singapore both favour administrative removal, trading speed for less prior judicial scrutiny. Malaysia's safeguards are mostly after the fact: judicial review and trial follow removal, whereas the UK places criminal thresholds before state action.

**Competing arguments.** The government says religion is volatile, viral content outruns courts, and licensed platforms can act fastest. Civil society, such as the Centre for Independent Journalism, answers that vague terms, executive takedowns and selective enforcement chill debate, and that harm should be shown before removal. Both are plausible here.

**Significance.** After the 2025 amendment, the 2026 Federal Court ruling and the Online Safety Act, enforcement is shifting from prosecuting speakers to governing platforms, leaving courts to review only charged cases.

## 4. Conclusion

The influencer's position is uncertain: s. 233 is stronger but turns on intent, while s. 298A depends on scope and faiths. TikTok must almost certainly comply and is unlikely to face criminal liability. In practice, removal occurs without adjudication.
