# Viral TikTok Video Mocking a Religious Group: The Legal Position of the Influencer and TikTok

*Incident report (ILAC plus Discussion). Law checked 23 October 2026.*

ILAC is a doctrinal method: it shows what the law says and how it applies ("law in the books"). It cannot show how the law works in practice or whether it is justified ("law in action"). The Discussion covers that, using one framework.

## Background

The scenario is a lecturer's hypothetical; no fact is confirmed. A Kuala Lumpur TikTok influencer posts a video mocking a religious group's practices. It goes viral. Police investigate under Penal Code s. 298A and Communications and Multimedia Act 1998 (Act 588) (CMA) s. 233. The Malaysian Communications and Multimedia Commission (MCMC) directs TikTok to remove it. No charge is stated. The religions of the influencer and the group, and the video's content, are not given; I flag where this matters. Stakeholders: influencer, TikTok, MCMC, police and Attorney General (AG), the religious group.

## I1: Penal Code s. 298A (influencer)

**Issue.** Could the video be an offence under s. 298A of the Penal Code (Act 574), given doubts about that section's validity and reach?

**Law.** Section 298A (in force) punishes causing, attempting or being likely to cause disharmony, enmity or ill will, or prejudicing harmony, on grounds of religion, by words, visible representations or otherwise. Reported penalty: two to five years' imprisonment [to verify]. Its validity is contested. In *Mamat bin Daud v Government of Malaysia* [1988] 1 MLJ 119 (SC) [citation to verify], a 3:2 majority held the section ultra vires (beyond Parliament's power) because it concerned Islam, a State List matter. In the *Alvivi* bloggers' case (Court of Appeal, 2014) [citation to verify], s. 298A(1) was reported as inapplicable to non-Muslims. In March 2025 the High Court upheld the related s. 298 (wounding religious feelings) against an Article 10 challenge (Malay Mail, 2025).

**Application.** The elements are (a) conduct by words or visual representation, (b) religion as the ground, and (c) a tendency to cause enmity or prejudice harmony. The video satisfies (a). Element (c) is contested. The prosecution would say virality shows likelihood, since no actual harm need be proved. The influencer would say mocking practices is not hatred of persons, and that the section states no mental element. The threshold problem is larger. If the influencer or the group is non-Muslim, *Mamat Daud* and *Alvivi* support an argument that s. 298A cannot apply. If the group is Muslim, the section's reach into State List matters remains doubtful, though the 2025 ruling on s. 298 shows courts are slow to strike down religion-protecting offences. Unlike *Alvivi*, where a specific halal-logo post was charged, the content here is unknown.

**Conclusion.** A s. 298A charge is arguable but legally fragile, depending on the parties' religions and the treatment of *Mamat Daud*.

## I2: CMA s. 233 and Article 10 (influencer)

**Issue.** Could the video be an offence under s. 233(1)(a) CMA, and is prosecution a permissible restriction under Article 10(2)(a) of the Federal Constitution?

**Law.** The Communications and Multimedia (Amendment) Act 2025 (in force 11 February 2025) changed "offensive" to "grossly offensive". Elements: (a) knowingly making and initiating transmission through network facilities, (b) grossly offensive content, (c) intent to annoy, abuse, threaten or harass another person (wording to verify). New Explanation 6 protects good-faith true statements of fact and opinion (Baker McKenzie, 2025). Reported penalty: fine up to RM500,000 and/or two years (Conventus Law, 2025) [to verify]. In *Government of Malaysia v Heidy Quah Gaik Li* (Civil Appeal No. 08(f)-35-02/2024(W)) (FC, 6 February 2026) [citation to verify], the Federal Court reversed the Court of Appeal and held "offensive" and "annoy" constitutional. It held that s. 233(1)(a) does not catch speech that is merely offensive or incidentally annoying, only communications whose primary purpose is annoyance, harm or distress (Skrine, 2026a). That case concerned the pre-amendment wording.

**Application.** Element (a) is met: the video was uploaded to TikTok. Element (b) is contested. The state would say ridiculing a faith's practices is grossly offensive; the influencer would invoke Explanation 6 and argue opinion or satire. Element (c) is decisive. The state would infer intent to annoy from tone, targeting and refusal to remove the video. The influencer would say the purpose was entertainment or critique and annoyance was a by-product; in *Heidy Quah* the prosecution failed on exactly this intent element. Virality shows effect, not purpose. On Article 10, the Federal Court accepted annoyance can fall within "public order", reversing the Court of Appeal's view that it was trivial (Skrine, 2026a). The real safeguard is the intent element.

**Conclusion.** Conviction is possible but unlikely on the facts given, because proving primary purpose is demanding. The pre-amendment words are constitutionally secure, and the amended wording will probably be treated alike.

## I3: TikTok

**Issue.** Must TikTok comply with MCMC's removal direction, and does it face liability itself?

**Law.** Social media services with at least eight million Malaysian users are deemed class-licensed applications service providers (Skrine, 2026b); TikTok's user count is assumed [to verify]. CMA s. 263(1) obliges licensees to prevent their networks being used for offences. The Online Safety Act 2025 (Act 866) (in force 1 January 2026) binds licensed providers, not individuals, and lets MCMC issue directions. Missing the periods set by the Online Safety (Period) Regulations 2025 for making harmful content inaccessible is an offence with a fine up to RM1 million. An aggrieved person may appeal to the Online Safety Appeal Tribunal (Skrine, 2026b).

**Application.** Section 233 targets whoever makes and initiates the transmission, so TikTok is not the offender while it is a conduit. Its exposure arises from non-compliance. The state would say a direction binds and delay is an offence. TikTok could argue the video breaches no community guideline, as it has before (about 86 per cent compliance, January to August 2025; The New Straits Times, 2025), or is not "harmful content" under the Act, which I could not verify for religious mockery. The Tribunal offers an appeal, but in practice platforms comply.

**Conclusion.** TikTok is likely bound to comply and has low liability if it does so promptly. Whether this content is "harmful content" is uncertain.

**Current status.** Investigation only (hypothetical). A charge rests with the AG (Art. 145(3)).

## Discussion

**Why the state regulates.** The justification is religious harmony and public order under Article 10(2)(a). Control is layered: police and AG (command-and-control), MCMC (state regulator), TikTok (platform governance).

**Theoretical lens: Habermas's public sphere.** Habermas (1989) describes a space where citizens debate common concerns by reasoned argument, free of state and market domination. It fits in three ways. It explains the state's claim: mockery is not rational-critical discourse, and enmity between faiths can drive a group out of the sphere. Habermas (2006) treats religious citizens as full participants, supporting protection of the group. Habermas (2022) argues platforms restructure the public sphere around commercial attention, which fits a case where virality, not argument, caused the harm and a private firm arbitrated the state's complaint. It fits poorly in two ways. It assumes a neutral state guarantor, but Malaysia's state is also guardian of religious sensitivity. And its deliberative standard is Western-derived and would classify nearly any viral satire as non-discourse.

| Who decides | Police, AG, MCMC, courts | Police, courts | Police, CPS, Ofcom [to verify] | Independent courts |
| Penalty | 2 to 5 years (s. 298A, reported); fine and/or 2 years (s. 233) | Up to 3 years or fine (reported) | Varies | Proportionate |
| Safeguards | Intent element; platform appeal to Tribunal | Judicial review | Free-speech balancing | Rabat Plan of Action |

I chose Singapore for its shared Penal Code heritage and religious-harmony priority; its offence requires knowing promotion, and the High Court upheld convictions over social media content. I chose the UK because Malaysia's "grossly offensive" echoes s. 127, which courts narrowed, and because its platform-duty model parallels the Online Safety Act. Malaysia's s. 298A sets the lowest mental-element threshold of the three.

**Competing arguments.** The government says viral religious ridicule can inflame a plural society fast, so platforms must act quickly. Critics say vague terms invite selective enforcement and that executive directions to private platforms bypass courts. The Court of Appeal in *Heidy Quah* made a version of that point before reversal.

**Significance.** The reform measures (the 2025 amendment, the Tribunal) sit beside wider enforcement and an upheld s. 233. The intent element now carries the weight of protecting speech.

## Conclusion

The influencer's exposure is uncertain under s. 298A and depends on proving primary purpose under s. 233. TikTok is likely bound to comply but is not the offender. Platform governance, not courts, decides the practical outcome.
