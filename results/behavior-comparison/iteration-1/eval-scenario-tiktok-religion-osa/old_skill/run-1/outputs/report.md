# Mocking a Religious Group on TikTok: The Legal Position of the Influencer and the Platform in Malaysia

*Incident report (hypothetical scenario). Law checked 23 October 2026.*

## Background and status

A Kuala Lumpur TikTok influencer posts a video mocking a religious group's practices. It goes viral. Police open an investigation under Penal Code (Act 574) s. 298A and Communications and Multimedia Act 1998 (Act 588) (CMA) s. 233. The Malaysian Communications and Multimedia Commission (MCMC) directs TikTok to remove the video.

These facts are the lecturer's assumptions, not a reported event. Nothing is confirmed beyond them: no charge, no court ruling, and the religion, the video's content and the influencer's intent are unknown. The status is **investigation only**. An investigation is not a finding of guilt. I assume the influencer is a citizen and that TikTok is licensed (see I2).

**Stakeholders:** the influencer; TikTok; MCMC and police (the Attorney General's Chambers decides prosecution); the religious group; the viewing public.

## I1: Influencer

**Issue.** Could the video amount to an offence under Penal Code s. 298A or CMA s. 233(1)(a), and would prosecution be a permissible restriction under Article 10(2)(a) of the Federal Constitution?

**Law.**
- *Constitution (in force).* Art. 10(1)(a) gives *every citizen* freedom of speech. Art. 10(2)(a) lets Parliament restrict it for security, public order, morality, defamation or incitement to an offence.
- *Penal Code s. 298A (in force).* Elements: (a) words, signs, visible representations or any conduct; (b) that cause, attempt to cause, or are *likely to cause* disharmony, enmity, hatred or ill will, or prejudice harmony, (c) on grounds of religion. Penalty: 2 to 5 years' imprisonment (BurgieLaw, n.d.; secondary text, verify against the official Act). Honest religious belief is no defence (s. 298A(7)), and courts accept the interpretation of a statutory religious authority (s. 298A(8)).
- *CMA s. 233 (amended; in force 11 February 2025).* The 2025 amendment replaced "offensive" with "grossly offensive", added Explanations, and raised the maximum to RM500,000 and/or 2 years (Law Partnership, 2025; Skrine, 2026). Elements: use of network facilities; content of the prohibited character; intent (to annoy, abuse, threaten or harass). [Exact amended wording to verify.]
- *Case law.* The Court of Appeal struck down "offensive" and "annoy" in the pre-amendment s. 233(1)(a) (Aug 2025). The Federal Court unanimously reversed this in *Government of Malaysia v Heidy Quah Gaik Li* (Civil Appeal No. 08(f)-35-02/2024(W)) (FC, reported February to March 2026; exact date and law report citation to verify). It held that speech intended to cause harm falls outside Art. 10(1)(a), and that annoyance can engage "public order" where race, religion or royalty is involved. It also read the offence down: the *mens rea* (mental element) must be a primary purpose to annoy, and cannot be inferred from offensiveness alone (Skrine, 2026).

**Application.** Under s. 233, use of network facilities is clearly met: the video was uploaded to TikTok. The contested elements are character and intent. The prosecution would argue that mockery of religious practice is "grossly offensive", and that repeated or targeted content shows a purpose to annoy. The influencer would argue that "grossly" is undefined, that the video was satire or commentary on practices (a public-interest purpose, which the Federal Court treated as lacking the primary intent), and that offence to viewers was a side effect. Because the Federal Court addressed only the old wording, how it applies to "grossly offensive" is untested.

Under s. 298A, the threshold is lower. Because "likely to cause" is enough, viral spread and public reaction help the prosecution, and no express intent is required on the text. The defence would argue that mocking *practices* is not enmity between *groups*, and that the likelihood must be real, not speculative. If the group is Muslim, s. 298A(8) lets religious authorities define the doctrine, which narrows the defence. Reported enforcement is severe: in 2019 a Kuching Sessions Court reportedly sentenced a Facebook user to ten years and ten months across charges under both provisions (CIVICUS Monitor, 2019).

On constitutionality, after the Federal Court's ruling a challenge based on Art. 10 alone is weak, and the court rejected proportionality as a free-standing test. Challenges are best raised in the trial court, not by separate suit.

**Conclusion.** A s. 298A offence is arguable and, if the video was widely seen, fairly likely to be made out on the elements. A s. 233 offence is arguable but turns on proof of intent. Prosecution is likely to survive an Art. 10 challenge. Uncertainty remains because there is no charge yet and the content is unknown.

## I2: TikTok

**Issue.** Is TikTok legally bound to comply with MCMC's removal direction, and what exposure follows if it delays or refuses?

**Law.**
- *Licensing (in force).* Platforms with at least eight million Malaysian users are deemed registered applications service provider licensees under CMA s. 46A from 1 January 2026 (Mayer Brown, 2026). [That TikTok exceeds the threshold is assumed.]
- *Online Safety Act 2025 (ONSA) (in force 1 January 2026).* It lists nine harmful-content categories, including content that may promote ill-will, hostility or disturbance of public tranquillity. After a report, a platform must acknowledge within one hour, assess within 12 hours, and make non-priority harmful content inaccessible within four hours of deciding not to dismiss it (permanently within 12 hours if confirmed). Non-compliance with MCMC directions carries fines up to RM1 million, with daily fines up to RM100,000. Failing a Part III duty carries a civil penalty up to RM10 million. Officers may be personally liable. An Online Safety Appeal Tribunal hears challenges; its decisions are final, and judicial review comes only afterwards (Mayer Brown, 2026).
- *Precedent for escalation.* On 21 May 2026 MCMC served TikTok a statutory demand for failing to moderate offensive content about the royal institution, requiring remedial measures and a formal explanation (The Star, 2026). [The CMA provision underlying individual takedown directions, possibly s. 263, to verify.]

**Application.** The state would argue that licensing makes TikTok a regulated gatekeeper with a "higher degree of responsibility" (The Star, 2026), so the direction binds it. TikTok could argue the direction must fit a statutory category, that "mocking practices" is not clearly "ill-will", and that over-removal harms users. Yet the tribunal route is narrow, and TikTok, as a foreign company, has no Art. 10(1)(a) right of its own. Its liability for the *content* is unlikely, as s. 233 and s. 298A target the maker. Its exposure lies in non-compliance or delay. Compliance with the direction is lawful, and removal does not decide the influencer's guilt.

**Conclusion.** TikTok is very likely bound to comply promptly. Refusal would risk fines and escalation; liability for the video itself is unlikely.

## Discussion

**Why the state regulates here.** The stated justifications are public order and religious harmony (the "3R" sensitivities of race, religion and royalty). The regulator is mixed: state command-and-control through police and MCMC, plus licence-based platform governance.

**Theoretical lens: Balkin's free speech triangle.** Balkin (2018) models speech as a triangle of states, private infrastructure owners and speakers. "Old-school" regulation punishes speakers; "new-school" regulation pressures infrastructure owners to police speech (Balkin, 2014). The scenario shows both at once: criminal investigation of the influencer (ss. 298A, 233) and a direction to TikTok. The lens explains why TikTok matters more than the influencer for suppressing a *viral* video: removal is faster than prosecution, and ONSA's hour-level deadlines reward quick removal over careful assessment (my inference). It also predicts collateral censorship, because a platform facing fines has little incentive to resist borderline takedowns. The fit is imperfect. Balkin wrote for liberal democracies, where state pressure on platforms is often informal; Malaysia formalises it through licences, statutory deadlines and penalties, so the "invisible" element is missing. The theory also says little about why religion is treated as a special category of public order.

**Comparison.** Singapore is chosen for shared legal heritage and a similar harmony rationale; the UK for a liberal, rights-based contrast.

Singapore shows that a harmony rationale can coexist with a *preventive* model that acts fast without a trial, with fewer procedural checks. The UK shows that religious hatred can be criminalised only where speech is threatening and intended, leaving mockery to be answered by counter-speech. Malaysia sits closer to Singapore on outcomes but, because of its broader "likely to cause" wording and heavy prison terms, nearer the restrictive end on thresholds.

**Competing arguments.** The government says a plural society needs preventive tools, and the Federal Court accepted that annoyance directed at religion may threaten public tranquillity; it also argues platforms profit from the content and must be accountable. Critics, including ARTICLE 19 (2025), say "offensive" and "grossly offensive" are vague, enforcement is arbitrary, and the result is self-censorship. They add that the new ONSA deadlines and final tribunal decisions push platforms to over-remove.

**Significance.** The Federal Court's ruling narrowed s. 233 through intent but kept it alive, while ONSA expanded platform control. The trajectory is double-track tightening: calibrated at the speaker end, firmer at the infrastructure end.

## Conclusion of the report

The influencer faces arguable liability under s. 298A and a contestable one under s. 233, with intent the pivotal question. TikTok must comply with MCMC's direction but is unlikely to be liable for the video. Through Balkin's triangle, the platform is now the main point of control.
