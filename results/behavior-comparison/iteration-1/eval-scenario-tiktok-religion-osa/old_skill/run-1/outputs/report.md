# Mocking a Religious Group on TikTok: The Legal Position of the Influencer and the Platform in Malaysia

*Hypothetical incident report; law checked 23 October 2026.*

## Background and status

A Kuala Lumpur TikTok influencer posts a video mocking a religious group's practices. It goes viral. Police open an investigation under Penal Code (Act 574) s. 298A and Communications and Multimedia Act 1998 (Act 588) (CMA) s. 233. The Malaysian Communications and Multimedia Commission (MCMC) directs TikTok to remove the video.

These are assumed facts, not a reported event. The religion, the video's content and the influencer's intent are unknown. Status: **investigation only**; no charge, no court ruling. I assume the influencer is a citizen and TikTok licensed. Stakeholders: the influencer, TikTok, MCMC, police, the Attorney General's Chambers (prosecution) and the religious group.

## I1: Influencer

**Issue.** Could the video amount to an offence under Penal Code s. 298A or CMA s. 233(1)(a), and would prosecution be a permissible restriction under Article 10(2)(a) of the Federal Constitution?

**Law.**
- *Constitution (in force).* Art. 10(1)(a) gives *every citizen* freedom of speech; Art. 10(2)(a) allows restrictions for security, public order, morality, defamation or incitement.
- *Penal Code s. 298A (in force).* Elements: (a) words, signs, visible representations or conduct; (b) that cause, attempt to cause, or are *likely to cause* disharmony, enmity, hatred or ill will; (c) on grounds of religion. Penalty: 2 to 5 years' imprisonment (BurgieLaw, n.d.; secondary text, verify). Honest religious belief is no defence (s. 298A(7)); courts accept the interpretation of a statutory religious authority (s. 298A(8)).
- *CMA s. 233 (amended; in force 11 February 2025).* "Offensive" became "grossly offensive", Explanations were added, and the maximum rose to RM500,000 and/or 2 years (Law Partnership, 2025; Skrine, 2026). Elements: network facilities; prohibited content; intent to annoy, abuse, threaten or harass. [Amended wording to verify.]
- *Case law.* The Court of Appeal struck down "offensive" and "annoy" in the old s. 233(1)(a) (August 2025). The Federal Court unanimously reversed it in *Government of Malaysia v Heidy Quah Gaik Li* (Civil Appeal No. 08(f)-35-02/2024(W)) (FC, early 2026; date and report citation to verify). It held that speech intended to cause harm is outside Art. 10(1)(a), and that annoyance over race, religion or royalty can engage "public order". It read the offence down: *mens rea* (the mental element) requires a primary purpose to annoy, not inferable from offensiveness alone (Skrine, 2026).

**Application.** Under s. 233, use of network facilities is clearly met: the video was uploaded to TikTok. Character and intent are contested. The prosecution would argue that mocking religious practice is "grossly offensive" and that targeting shows a purpose to annoy. The influencer would argue that "grossly" is undefined, that the video was satire or commentary, and that offence was a side effect, the kind of secondary effect the Federal Court said does not satisfy intent. The Federal Court decided only the old wording; "grossly offensive" is untested.

Under s. 298A the threshold is lower. "Likely to cause" suffices, so viral spread helps the prosecution, and the text has no express intent element. The defence would argue that mocking *practices* is not enmity between *groups*, and that likelihood must be real, not speculative. If the group is Muslim, s. 298A(8) narrows the defence further. Enforcement can be severe: a Kuching Sessions Court reportedly sentenced a Facebook user in 2019 to ten years and ten months across charges including both provisions (CIVICUS Monitor, 2019).

On constitutionality, the Federal Court treated proportionality as no free-standing test and found such restrictions within "public order", so an Art. 10 challenge looks weak.

**Conclusion.** A s. 298A offence is arguable and, given wide circulation, fairly likely on the elements. A s. 233 offence is arguable but turns on proof of intent. Prosecution would probably survive an Art. 10 challenge. Uncertainty remains: no charge, content unknown.

## I2: TikTok

**Issue.** Is TikTok legally bound to comply with MCMC's removal direction, and what exposure follows from delay or refusal?

**Law.**
- *Licensing (in force).* Platforms with at least eight million Malaysian users are deemed registered application service providers under CMA s. 46A from 1 January 2026 (Mayer Brown, 2026). [TikTok meeting this threshold is assumed.]
- *Online Safety Act 2025 (ONSA) (in force 1 January 2026).* Its harmful-content categories include content that may promote ill-will, hostility or disturbance of public tranquillity. For non-priority harmful content, a platform must acknowledge a report within one hour, assess within 12 hours, make content inaccessible within four hours of deciding not to dismiss, and remove it permanently within 12 hours if confirmed. Breaching MCMC directions or takedown periods carries fines up to RM1 million (daily fines up to RM100,000); failing a Part III duty, a civil penalty up to RM10 million. Officers may be personally liable. An Appeal Tribunal hears challenges; its decisions are final, and judicial review comes only afterwards (Mayer Brown, 2026).
- *Escalation precedent.* On 21 May 2026 MCMC served TikTok a statutory demand for failing to moderate offensive content about the royal institution (The Star, 2026). [CMA provision behind individual takedown directions, possibly s. 263, to verify.]

**Application.** The state would argue that licensing makes TikTok a gatekeeper with "a higher degree of responsibility" (MCMC, in The Star, 2026), so the direction binds it. TikTok could argue the direction must fit a statutory category and that mockery is not clearly "ill-will". But the tribunal route is narrow, and a foreign company probably has no Art. 10(1)(a) right of its own. Liability for the *content* is unlikely, since ss. 298A and 233 target the maker. Exposure lies in delay or non-compliance.

**Conclusion.** TikTok is very likely bound to comply promptly. Refusal risks fines and escalation; liability for the video is unlikely.

## Discussion

**Why the state regulates here.** The justifications are public order and religious harmony (the "3R" sensitivities: race, religion, royalty). The regulator is mixed: police and MCMC command-and-control, plus licence-based platform governance.

**Theoretical lens: Balkin's free speech triangle.** Balkin (2018) models speech as a triangle of states, private infrastructure owners and speakers. "Old-school" regulation punishes speakers; "new-school" regulation pressures infrastructure owners to police speech (Balkin, 2014). The scenario shows both: investigating the influencer and directing TikTok. The lens explains why TikTok matters most for a *viral* video: removal is faster than prosecution, and ONSA's hour-level deadlines favour quick removal over careful assessment (my inference). It also predicts collateral censorship, since a fined platform has little reason to resist borderline takedowns. The fit is imperfect. Balkin wrote for liberal democracies, where state pressure on platforms is often informal; Malaysia formalises it through licences and penalties. It also cannot explain why religion is a special public-order category.

**Comparison.** Singapore: shared heritage, similar harmony rationale. UK: liberal, rights-based contrast.

| Dimension | Malaysia | Singapore | UK | ICCPR Art. 19/20 |

Singapore pairs a harmony rationale with *preventive*, executive-led tools. The UK criminalises religious hatred only where speech is threatening and intended, leaving mockery to counter-speech. Malaysia's "likely to cause" wording and prison minimum place it at the restrictive end.

**Competing arguments.** The government says a plural society needs preventive tools; the Federal Court accepted annoyance over religion can threaten public tranquillity, and MCMC expects platforms to act on 3R content (The Star, 2026). Critics, including ARTICLE 19 (2025), say "offensive" and "grossly offensive" are vague, enforcement is arbitrary, and self-censorship results; strict deadlines and final tribunal decisions may push platforms to over-remove.

**Significance.** The Federal Court narrowed s. 233 through intent but kept it alive, while ONSA expanded platform control. The trajectory is double-track tightening: intent-based care for speakers, speed for platforms.

## Conclusion of the report

The influencer faces arguable liability under s. 298A and a contestable case under s. 233, with intent pivotal. TikTok must comply with MCMC's direction but is unlikely to be liable for the video. Through Balkin's triangle, the platform is the main control point.
