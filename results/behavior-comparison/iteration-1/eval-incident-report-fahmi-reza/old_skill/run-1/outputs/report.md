# The Fahmi Reza "Clown" Caricature of Najib Razak and Prosecution under Section 233 of the Communications and Multimedia Act 1998: An Incident Report

*COM741 Media Law and Regulations. Mode B (incident report). Status of facts and law checked 23 October 2026.*

---

## 1. Background and timeline

**Confirmed (reported consistently by mainstream outlets and NGOs):**

- **Early 2016.** Graphic artist and activist Fahmi Reza posted a caricature of then Prime Minister Najib Razak with a clown's face. The image circulated widely online. Police publicly warned internet users about sharing such images (Global Voices, 2016). Fahmi was investigated under s.233 of the Communications and Multimedia Act 1998 (CMA) (Act 588) and reportedly also under s.504 of the Penal Code. A pro-government group, Gerakan Merah, is reported to have lodged a police report. This complaint is reported, not independently confirmed here.
- **March 2016.** Fahmi was reportedly placed on a wanted list and an immigration blacklist.
- **June 2016.** Fahmi was charged under s.233(1)(a) CMA in two places, Ipoh and Kuala Lumpur. The Ipoh charge concerned a Facebook post of the clown image alongside the logo of the Malaysian Communications and Multimedia Commission (MCMC), reported as posted in February 2016. He claimed trial to both charges. In the same month he was questioned by police under the Sedition Act 1948 over "#KitaSemuaPenghasut" T-shirts bearing the image. The exact date of his arrest or detention was not found in the sources consulted [to verify].
- **August 2016.** Fahmi applied to challenge the constitutionality of the charge under Art. 10 (Free Malaysia Today, 2016). The High Court referred the question upward. A five-member Federal Court bench later set the referral aside as improperly brought, citing non-compliance with the Courts of Judicature Act 1964, and sent the matter back to the Sessions Court. The date of that ruling is [to verify]. The constitutional question was therefore not decided on its merits in his case.
- **20 February 2018.** The Ipoh Sessions Court (Judge Norashima Khalid) convicted him. It found the defence had not raised reasonable doubt, and imposed one month's jail and a RM30,000 fine. The sentence was stayed pending appeal (bail RM10,000).
- **October 2018.** After the change of government (May 2018), the prosecution withdrew the Kuala Lumpur charge and the Sessions Court discharged him (New Straits Times, 2018a).
- **12 November 2018.** The Ipoh High Court (Judicial Commissioner Mohd Radzi Harun) upheld the conviction but set aside the jail term and reduced the fine to RM10,000, with one month's jail in default. The judge reportedly cited the KL withdrawal and the government's stated plan to amend s.233 (New Straits Times, 2018b; Malay Mail, 2018).
- **January 2019.** The prosecution did not pursue a heavier sentence (Free Malaysia Today, 2019).
- **5 July 2019.** The Court of Appeal in Putrajaya (three judges, led by Justice Kamardin Hashim) unanimously dismissed his appeal and upheld the conviction (Malay Mail, 2019). Frontline Defenders and Malay Mail describe the upheld sentence slightly differently [to verify against the written grounds].

**Source conflicts and gaps.** Reports differ on the maximum penalty under the old s.233 (one report says up to one year or RM50,000, another says up to two years). The statute's own text says RM50,000 or one year or both, so I use that [to verify against the 2024 reprint]. I did not find the written grounds of any of the three courts, nor any record of a further appeal to the Federal Court.

## 2. Stakeholders

- **Speaker.** Fahmi Reza, a political artist who described the work as satire of alleged corruption (the 1MDB affair).
- **Complainant and subject.** Najib Razak, then Prime Minister, and a complainant group (Gerakan Merah).
- **Enforcers.** The Royal Malaysia Police, MCMC (whose logo featured in the image) and the Attorney General's Chambers (AGC), which holds prosecutorial discretion under Art. 145(3) of the Federal Constitution.
- **Courts.** Sessions Court, High Court, Court of Appeal and Federal Court.
- **Civil society.** Lawyers for Liberty, Aliran and Frontline Defenders criticised the conviction. Article 19 has repeatedly urged review of the CMA.

## 3. ILAC analysis

### Issue 1 (offence): Could the clown caricature, posted online, constitute an offence under s.233(1)(a) CMA as it stood in 2016?

**Law.** *Status: s.233(1)(a) in its 2016 wording was amended with effect from 11 February 2025; the 2016 wording governed this case.* The old s.233(1)(a) criminalised a person who "knowingly" (i) makes, creates or solicits and (ii) initiates the transmission of any comment, request, suggestion or other communication (iii) that is "obscene, indecent, false, menacing or offensive in character" (iv) "with intent to annoy, abuse, threaten or harass another person", (v) by means of network facilities or network service or applications service. Intent is the *mens rea* (the mental element). The penalty under s.233(3) was a fine up to RM50,000 or imprisonment up to one year or both, with a further daily fine for a continuing offence (Communications and Multimedia Act 1998 (Act 588), s. 233).

**Application.** Elements (i), (ii) and (v) are not seriously contested: Fahmi created and posted the image on Facebook, a network application service. The contest lies in elements (iii) and (iv). On one hand, the prosecution would argue that depicting the Prime Minister as a clown, here beside the MCMC logo, is "offensive in character" because it ridicules and demeans, and that intent to annoy can be inferred from the targeting of a named person and from Fahmi's continued circulation, including T-shirts, after the police warning. The Sessions Court accepted this and found no reasonable doubt. On the other hand, the defence would argue that "offensive" is undefined and subjective, that satire of a public figure's conduct is not offensive "in character" in the way obscenity or menace is, and that the dominant purpose was political criticism, not to annoy Najib personally. Annoyance may be a foreseeable by-product of political satire without being its intent. Truth or public interest is not a defence under s.233, which narrows the defence's room. Three courts upheld the conviction, so as a matter of Malaysian law the elements were found to be met.

**Conclusion.** It is likely, and it was in fact held at three court levels, that the post fell within the 2016 wording of s.233(1)(a). The result nonetheless depended on an undefined term ("offensive") and an inferred intent, so it is arguable that a differently reasoned court could have acquitted.

### Issue 2 (state action): Was the investigation and prosecution lawful in power and procedure?

**Law.** Police may investigate a seizable or arrestable offence under the Criminal Procedure Code. The AGC, as Public Prosecutor, has control over prosecution under Art. 145(3), which courts review only narrowly. Appeals run from Sessions Court to High Court to Court of Appeal.

**Application.** The charge was brought under a valid, in-force statute, before competent courts, and was tested through the full ordinary appeal chain. Procedurally, therefore, the prosecution appears lawful. Contested points are about discretion, not power. The defence side would point to selective enforcement: the case was brought under the Barisan Nasional government against a critic of its Prime Minister, a second parallel charge was later dropped under the new government, and the High Court itself referred to the intended amendment of s.233 as a reason to reduce the sentence. The state would answer that prosecutorial discretion is the AGC's, and that its later withdrawal in KL reflects a policy change, not unlawfulness in the original charge. The parallel Sedition Act questioning and the travel restriction are separate state actions I could not assess in detail. The police later described one travel ban as a mistake (Asia News Network, 2017) [to verify].

**Conclusion.** The prosecution was most likely procedurally lawful, since the power, forum and appeal route were all valid. Whether the decision to charge was appropriate is a policy and discretion question that the courts did not review, so no finding of unlawfulness is supportable on the available record.

### Issue 3 (constitutionality): Is criminalising "offensive" communication "with intent to annoy" a permissible restriction on freedom of expression under Art. 10(2)(a) of the Federal Constitution, and was it so applied to Fahmi?

**Law.** Art. 10(1)(a) guarantees freedom of speech and expression. Art. 10(2)(a) permits Parliament to impose restrictions "necessary or expedient" in the interest of security, friendly relations with other countries, public order or morality, and to protect Parliamentary privileges, or against contempt of court, defamation or incitement to an offence. Art. 8 (equality) also applies. Malaysian courts have said such restrictions must be reasonable and proportionate, not merely any restriction that Parliament deems expedient (*Sivarasa Rasiah v Badan Peguam Malaysia* [2010] 2 MLJ 333 (FC) [citation to verify]). Malaysia is not a party to the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR), so Art. 19 ICCPR is persuasive only. Two recent decisions in *Heidy Quah* are central. On **19 August 2025** the Court of Appeal held the words "offensive" and "intent to annoy" in s.233(1)(a) inconsistent with Arts. 8 and 10, reportedly reasoning that such speech is not a threat to public order and that the provision is vague and lacks a truth defence. On **6 February 2026** a five-member Federal Court bench (including Justice Nallini Pathmanathan) unanimously reversed that part, holding the words constitutional. It reasoned that striking them would leave people exposed to communications made with specific intent to cause annoyance, harm or distress, which can be regulated. In the same case it held Quah's prosecution wrong because her post of fact and opinion was protected by Art. 10(1)(a) and was neither offensive nor made with intent to annoy (Malay Mail, 2026; Bernama, 2026) [full judgment and neutral citation to verify].

**Application.** The *ratio decidendi* (binding reason) of the Federal Court is that the statutory words are constitutionally valid. That holding is binding on lower courts and answers any facial challenge (a challenge to the law itself) of the kind Fahmi tried to raise in 2016. On one hand, the state's justification is that harassment-type speech with intent to annoy is a legitimate ground for regulation, and that the 2025 amendment ("grossly offensive") narrows the offence further. On the other hand, critics argue the 2016 wording lacked a clear public-order or morality link, and that the Court of Appeal's reasoning (2025) remains the more rights-protective reading. For an as-applied challenge (whether the law was constitutional in Fahmi's circumstances), the Federal Court's *obiter dicta* (remarks not essential to the result) about the protected status of political opinion and its insistence on proof of real offensiveness and intent are significant. A satirical caricature of the Prime Minister on a matter of public concern, now set against Najib's later corruption convictions, is plausibly closer to Quah's protected opinion than to targeted harassment. But a clown image is also more plainly insulting than Quah's factual post, so the analogy is not clean. Fahmi's own constitutional point was never decided on the merits (see Section 1).

**Conclusion.** The statutory wording applied to Fahmi is, on current Federal Court authority, constitutional on its face. Whether his conviction was a proportionate application is arguable and unresolved; I consider it open to serious doubt after *Heidy Quah* but I found no record that he has sought to reopen it.

## 4. Current status (checked 23 October 2026)

**Fahmi's case.** Conviction upheld by the Court of Appeal (5 July 2019). I found no later appeal or reopening [to verify with eJudgment or the Bar Council].

**How s.233 has changed.**

| Date | Change | Status |
|---|---|---|
| 1998 | CMA enacted; s.233 covers improper use of network facilities | In force |
| 2016 wording | "obscene, indecent, false, menacing or offensive ... intent to annoy, abuse, threaten or harass"; fine up to RM50,000 or 1 year, or both | Superseded |
| Nov 2018 | Government says it will review/amend s.233 (cited by the High Court) | Policy statement only |
| 11 Feb 2025 | Communications and Multimedia (Amendment) Act 2025: "offensive" becomes "grossly offensive"; added intent to commit fraud or dishonesty; penalty up to RM500,000 or 2 years (RM5,000 daily fine; up to 5 years where a minor is involved); explanatory definitions added [text of the Explanations to verify] | In force (all but two unrelated sections: unsolicited commercial messages and communications-data preservation) |
| 19 Aug 2025 | Court of Appeal in *Heidy Quah* strikes down old "offensive"/"annoy" wording; does not rule on the amended text | Reversed on this point |
| 6 Feb 2026 | Federal Court restores the words; holds them constitutional | Binding authority |

The net effect is that the government kept s.233, raised the threshold of the offensiveness limb ("grossly") but sharply raised penalties tenfold on the fine, and the apex court has endorsed the provision's constitutionality while signalling limits in application. Parallel reforms include the Online Safety Act 2025 and a platform-licensing regime [status to verify].

**Najib.** He was later convicted in the SRC International case (final at the Federal Court in 2022 [to verify]). His sentence was reduced by the Pardons Board in 2024 (reported as six years, RM50 million fine). He was reportedly sentenced to 15 years in the 1MDB case in December 2025 and is appealing. On 18 September 2026 he was reportedly granted a conditional pardon to serve the rest of his SRC term under house arrest until 23 August 2028, subject to paying the fine. Reports conflict on whether the order has taken effect [to verify]. A 14 October 2026 High Court ruling reportedly denied a stay of the 1MDB term.

## 5. Discussion

**Why the state regulates here.** The stated justifications are public order, protection of reputation and dignity, and prevention of online harassment. The regulator is state command-and-control: MCMC and the police investigate, the AGC prosecutes, and courts decide. There is no self-regulatory buffer for an individual's post. The old wording's breadth (undefined "offensive") made it available for political use, which is the core concern in this incident.

**Theoretical lens.** Two frameworks fit. First, Habermas's public sphere: satire is a low-cost, widely accessible form of participation, and prosecuting a caricaturist of the head of government narrows who may speak and how, shaping rational-critical debate toward deference. Second, the authoritarian and libertarian poles of Siebert, Peterson and Schramm's (1956) normative press theories help to place the state's reasoning: the prosecution protects authority and "harmony", while the defence invokes a libertarian marketplace of ideas. These theories fit poorly, however. They were built to classify press systems and owners, not individual online satirists or platform-mediated speech. They also treat "Malaysia" as a fixed type, whereas this case shows its position moving with changes of government (BN prosecution, Pakatan withdrawal, Unity Government amendment). Hallin and Mancini's models are not used because they classify Western media systems and do not address individual speech offences.

**Comparative analysis.** The comparators were chosen because each has (or had) a near-identical "grossly offensive"/annoyance offence, so differences come from court responses, not drafting.

| Dimension | Malaysia | India | United Kingdom | International standard (ICCPR Art. 19) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Legal basis | CMA 1998 s.233 (amended 2025) | Information Technology Act 2000 s.66A (repealed by judicial strike-down) | Communications Act 2003 s.127 [current status after the Online Safety Act 2023 to verify] | Art. 19(3) ICCPR; General Comment 34 (2011) |
| Threshold for restriction | "Grossly offensive" with intent to annoy (post-2025); Federal Court (2026) upholds the words | s.66A used "grossly offensive" and "annoyance"; struck down as vague and not within Art. 19(2) grounds (*Shreya Singhal v Union of India* (2015) 5 SCC 1 [to verify]) | "Grossly offensive" read narrowly; a "joke" lacking menace is not covered (*Chambers v DPP* [2012] EWHC 2157 (Admin) [to verify]) | Legality, legitimate aim, necessity, proportionality |
| Who decides / regulator | Police, MCMC, AGC, courts | Police, courts | Police, CPS, courts | Independent judiciary |
| Penalty / remedy | Up to RM500,000 or 2 years | Up to 3 years (when in force) | Up to 6 months (s.127) [to verify] | Criminal sanction for criticism of public figures disfavoured (GC 34) |
| Safeguards | Narrowing by courts; no truth/public-interest defence; Art. 10(1)(a) as read in *Heidy Quah* | Strike-down | Human Rights Act 1998 and ECHR Art. 10 (*Handyside v UK* (1976): protects speech that "offends, shocks or disturbs") | Public figures tolerate broader criticism |

India shows a court striking down the equivalent text outright; the UK shows the same words surviving but being read down to require menace or real gravity; Malaysia's Federal Court took a third path, upholding the words while limiting their use through application. That makes the Malaysian outcome more dependent on prosecutorial and judicial restraint, which was exactly what failed in Fahmi's case in 2018 and 2019 but, on the Federal Court's reasoning, should now be stronger. A Southeast Asian comparator (for example Singapore's Protection from Harassment Act 2014 and POFMA 2019, or Thailand's lèse-majesté provisions) would show the regional pattern of broad speech offences with executive control, but I did not verify current provisions and omit it.

**Competing arguments.** The government's case is that online anonymity and virality justify a harassment-type offence, that the 2025 amendment ("grossly") and the Explanations add precision, and that the Federal Court confirmed the law's validity while shielding genuine opinion. The civil society case (Article 19; Lawyers for Liberty; Aliran) is that the offence is vague, lacks a truth or public-interest defence, was used against a political critic, and that a tenfold rise in penalties after the amendment increases the chilling effect on satire. Both positions have force: the Federal Court's rejection of Quah's charge supports the government's point that the courts can correct misuse, while Fahmi's three-court conviction supports the critics' point that correction came too late for him.

**Significance.** The incident shows that Malaysian media-law reform has followed a pattern of promised review (2018), delayed amendment (2025) that raised penalties rather than repealing the provision, and judicial rather than legislative clarification (2025-2026). It also shows that the Art. 10 protection of political satire currently rests on case-by-case application rather than on a clear statutory defence.

## 6. Conclusion of the report

Fahmi Reza's prosecution was procedurally lawful, and his conviction under the 2016 wording of s.233(1)(a) CMA was upheld by three courts. The words applied to him are, as of the Federal Court's 6 February 2026 ruling, constitutional on their face, although the same ruling's emphasis on protected opinion makes the fairness of applying them to political satire open to serious doubt. Section 233 has since been narrowed in wording ("grossly offensive") but made much harsher in penalty. The trajectory suggests that protection for satire will depend on how prosecutors and judges apply the law, not on legislative reform.
