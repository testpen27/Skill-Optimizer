# Incident Report: The Clown Caricature of Najib Razak (2016) and the Prosecution of Fahmi Reza under Section 233 of the Communications and Multimedia Act 1998

**Mode:** Incident report (the incident reached court, so court outcomes are included). **Status checked:** 23 October 2026. **Prepared for:** COM741 Media Law and Regulations.

**How to read this report.** The first half uses ILAC (Issue, Law, Application, Conclusion), a doctrinal method that answers what the law is and how it applies, the "law in the books". ILAC cannot show whom the law falls on in practice or whether it is justified, so the second half is a Discussion that moves to the "law in action" using media theory and comparison.

---

## 1. Background and timeline

**Confirmed (from court reports and news agencies):**

- In early 2016, while Najib Razak was prime minister, graphic artist Mohd Fahmi Reza Mohd Zarin posted an image depicting Najib with a clown's face, together with the logo of the Malaysian Communications and Multimedia Commission (MCMC) (New Straits Times [NST], 2018a).
- He was charged on 10 June 2016 in Ipoh under s. 233(1)(a) of the Communications and Multimedia Act 1998 (Act 588) (CMA) for posting the image on his Facebook page on 8 February 2016, said to have been viewed at a commercial address in Ipoh (NST, 2018a; Malay Mail, 2018a). He pleaded not guilty.
- A second, similar charge was brought in Kuala Lumpur (Free Malaysia Today [FMT], 2016, as summarised in search results; the article itself could not be opened). It was dropped on 11 October 2018 after the prosecution applied to withdraw it (NST, 2018b).
- **20 February 2018:** the Ipoh Sessions Court (Judge Norashima Khalid) found the defence had not raised a reasonable doubt and sentenced him to one month's jail and a RM30,000 fine (six months' jail in default). Sentence was stayed pending appeal (NST, 2018a).
- **12 November 2018:** the Ipoh High Court (Mohd Radzi Harun) upheld the conviction but set aside the jail term and reduced the fine to RM10,000. The judge said he was aware of media reports that the Ministry intended to amend s. 233(1)(a), and he took into account the Kuala Lumpur withdrawal (Malay Mail, 2018a).
- **Dec 2018 to Jan 2019:** the prosecution discontinued its appeal for a heavier sentence (FMT, 2019, from search summary).
- **5 July 2019:** a three-judge Court of Appeal panel led by Kamardin Hashim dismissed Fahmi's appeal as without merit and upheld conviction and sentence (Front Line Defenders, n.d.; Malay Mail, 2019, from search summaries).

**Not confirmed or in conflict:**

- Exact date of first posting and any police or MCMC investigation or arrest before the 10 June 2016 charge: not established from the sources I could open. [to verify]
- Sources differ on the offence date of the second charge (1 February 2016 in one NST report; 8 February for the Ipoh charge) and on which limb of the s. 233(1)(a) list was particularised. Malay Mail describes "false communication content ... with the intention to offend", while Reuters, quoted by NST, lists "obscene, indecent, false, menacing or offensive" (the statutory list). The charge sheet itself was not seen.
- Who the "complainants" were (the High Court refers to them) is not stated in the sources. [to verify]

## 2. Stakeholders

- **Speaker:** Fahmi Reza, a political activist and designer, represented by Syahredzan Johan (Malay Mail, 2018a).
- **Target:** Najib Razak, then prime minister and head of the government that oversaw MCMC.
- **Regulator and prosecutor:** MCMC, whose own logo appears in the image. MCMC prosecutors (Mohd Sophian Zakaria and Nazrul Nizam Zameri) conducted the case (NST, 2018a).
- **Courts:** Sessions Court, High Court, Court of Appeal.
- **Later actors:** the Pakatan Harapan government (2018), whose Communications Minister Gobind Singh Deo said he would raise the conviction with the Attorney General and referred to a moratorium on s. 233 enforcement (Malay Mail, 2018b, from search summary); civil society (Aliran, SUARAM, Article 19); and Parliament, which amended s. 233 in 2025.
- **Platform:** Facebook (Meta). No platform duty was in issue in 2016.

## 3. ILAC analysis

### Issue 1: Was the prosecution lawful under s. 233(1)(a) as it stood in 2016?

**Issue.** Whether posting a clown caricature of the prime minister on Facebook satisfied the elements of s. 233(1)(a) of the CMA 1998, so that the prosecution and conviction were lawful.

**Law.** *Status: s. 233 amended; the 2016 wording was in force in 2016 and has since been replaced (see Section 4).* As it stood in 2016, s. 233(1)(a) made it an offence for a person, by means of network facilities, network service or applications service, to *knowingly* (i) make, create or solicit and (ii) initiate the transmission of any comment, request, suggestion or other communication which is "obscene, indecent, false, menacing or offensive in character" with "intent to annoy, abuse, threaten or harass another person". The maximum penalty was a fine of RM50,000 or one year's imprisonment or both (Malay Mail, 2018a). This wording is reconstructed from secondary reports and should be checked against a pre-2025 reprint of Act 588. [to verify]

Elements: (a) use of network facilities or services (the means); (b) knowingly creating and initiating transmission (the act and knowledge); (c) content "offensive in character" (the character of the content); (d) *mens rea*, the mental element, here intent to annoy, abuse, threaten or harass "another person" (the target). Satire is not a statutory defence.

**Application.**

*(a) and (b), the means and the act.* These are not disputed. The image was uploaded to Fahmi's own Facebook page, which is a network or applications service, and he created it. The Ipoh charge added that the content was viewed at an Ipoh address, which supplied the place of offence.

*(c) "Offensive in character".* The state's case is straightforward: depicting a head of government as a clown, branded with the regulator's logo, is derogatory and, on its face, offensive. The High Court judge relied on "judicial notice", meaning facts a court accepts as commonly known without proof (Malay Mail, 2018a), and, according to a critical commentary, said Fahmi had forgotten his "manners as a Malay" (Surendran, 2018). The defence had the better of the textual argument: "offensive" is undefined and measured by no objective standard. Mocking a politician is offensive to the politician almost by definition, as Surendran (2018) observes, so if offensiveness alone suffices, almost any political caricature becomes criminal. The defence also argued that the charge was defective because it named no person or party who was annoyed (Front Line Defenders, n.d.). No court accepted this.

*(d) Intent to annoy.* This is the contested element and the real question. The prosecution would argue that the target's identity, the clown imagery and the mock use of the MCMC logo support an inference that the primary aim was to demean. Fahmi argued that the image was parody criticising the authorities for suppressing communication on social media (Front Line Defenders, n.d.), so the dominant purpose was political commentary, with any annoyance incidental. The courts below, as reported, treated the offensive nature and the circumstances as sufficient and found the offence proved beyond reasonable doubt. The reported reasoning does not show how intent was separated from offensiveness. That gap matters because the Federal Court later held (Issue 2) that intent cannot be inferred from offensiveness alone.

*Comparison with decided cases.* The only authority directly on point is the series of decisions in Fahmi's own case, all of which went against him. Unlike *Government of Malaysia v Heidy Quah Gaik Li* (Federal Court, 2026), where a Facebook post on detention conditions was held to be fact and opinion on a matter of public concern, the Fahmi image makes no factual claim. It is, however, closer to the protected category of "satire" that the Federal Court named than to the targeted harassment, doxxing or trolling it said falls within s. 233(1)(a).

**Conclusion.** On the law as courts then applied it, the prosecution was lawful: three levels of court found the elements met. The weakest link is the intent element, where the reported reasoning leans on offensiveness. Under the intent-focused reading adopted in 2026, the conviction would be difficult to sustain. Certainty: moderate, because I did not read the judgments themselves.

### Issue 2: Was the restriction constitutional under Article 10?

**Issue.** Whether applying s. 233(1)(a) to a political caricature was a permissible restriction on freedom of speech under Articles 10(1)(a) and 10(2)(a) of the Federal Constitution.

**Law.** *Status: Federal Constitution in force.* Article 10(1)(a) guarantees every citizen freedom of speech and expression, subject to Article 10(2)(a), under which Parliament may impose by law restrictions it "deems necessary or expedient" in the interest of the security of the Federation, public order or morality, or to provide against defamation or incitement to an offence. Article 4(1) makes the Constitution supreme and voids inconsistent laws; Article 4(2) purports to bar challenges to such restrictions. Key authority, with what it decided:

- *Government of Malaysia v Heidy Quah Gaik Li* (Civil Appeal No. 08(f)-35-02/2024(W)), Federal Court, 6 February 2026 (unanimous; reported at [2026] 2 MLJ 397 [citation to verify]). The court reversed the Court of Appeal (19 August 2025), which had struck down "offensive" and "annoy" as unconstitutional. It held: Article 4(2) is not an absolute bar to review; Article 10(1)(a) does not protect speech intended to cause harm or emotional distress; the provision falls within the "public order" ground; proportionality is an interpretive aid, not a free-standing test (distinguishing *Sivarasa Rasiah v Badan Peguam Malaysia* [2010] 2 MLJ 333); the words are saved by "reading down", the core of the offence being a specific intent to annoy, which "cannot be inferred solely from the offensive nature of the communication"; and political, social or satirical speech whose primary purpose is not to cause harm stays protected (Skrine, 2026). The court also said the challenge should have been raised in the pending criminal proceedings, not by a separate suit after the charge was discharged (Skrine, 2026).
- *Public Prosecutor v Azmi Sharom* [2015] 6 MLJ 751 (FC): the Sedition Act was upheld against an Article 10 challenge (from the status file; not re-read).

**Application.**

*Is the restriction on a permitted ground?* The government would say s. 233(1)(a) protects public order, because offensive online messages sent to annoy can escalate in a plural society, and the Federal Court accepted this for the provision in general (Skrine, 2026). Against that, a caricature of a prime minister raises no apparent race, religion or royalty sensitivity, the settings the Federal Court mainly had in mind. Critics argue that using criminal law to shield a politician from ridicule protects a reputational interest, which Article 10(2)(a) addresses only through defamation, and no defamation claim was brought (Surendran, 2018). On that view the application, not the section, is the problem.

*Does the provision survive on its face?* After February 2026, yes. The Federal Court's ruling closes the facial argument that Fahmi's supporters would have made in 2018. The Court of Appeal's 2019 decision therefore stands on firmer ground as to the section's validity, though not necessarily as to its application.

*Was the section validly applied to Fahmi?* The Federal Court's interpretive gloss is the key. If satire with a primary political purpose is outside s. 233(1)(a), the question becomes whether Fahmi's primary purpose was to annoy. On one hand, the state can point to the mock regulator logo and to the image's sole function of ridiculing a named individual. On the other hand, the image circulated in the context of public debate about a sitting prime minister, and the defence characterised it as a protest about censorship. The 2026 ruling is not a retroactive review of Fahmi's case. His conviction was affirmed by the Court of Appeal in 2019, and I could not verify whether any further appeal was sought or whether an Article 10 argument was raised then. [to verify]

*International standard.* Malaysia is not a party to the ICCPR, so Article 19 is persuasive only. The UN Human Rights Committee has said that insult to a public figure does not by itself justify penalties (General Comment 34, para. 38 [to verify]).

**Conclusion.** Section 233(1)(a) is facially constitutional after the Federal Court's 2026 ruling. Whether the prosecution of Fahmi was a constitutionally permissible application is arguable and, on the 2026 reasoning, probably not: a charge resting on offensiveness alone would now be unlikely to survive. Certainty is moderate because no court has re-examined his conviction against the 2026 test.

### Issue 3: Was the enforcement process fair and proportionate?

**Issue.** Whether the manner of enforcement (prosecution by the regulator whose logo was used, two parallel charges, and the sentence) was proper.

**Law.** Prosecutorial discretion lies with the Attorney General as Public Prosecutor (Federal Constitution, Art. 145(3)). The Criminal Procedure Code allows withdrawal of a charge (s. 254 was applied in the Kuala Lumpur case, per NST, 2018b). Sentencing within the statutory maximum is for the court.

**Application.** On one hand, the prosecution acted within its powers: it proceeded in Ipoh, withdrew in Kuala Lumpur, and later declined to press for a higher sentence, which shows discretion was exercised. On the other hand, the regulator prosecuted a case in which its own logo had been used to mock the executive, which at least raises a perception of institutional interest. Two charges for the same image in two courts also increased the burden on the accused. The first-instance sentence (jail plus RM30,000) was reduced because of a policy signal (a planned amendment) and a related charge being withdrawn, not because of a legal error. That suggests the courts treated the law as valid but its application as harsh. The Minister's later reference to a moratorium on s. 233 enforcement, with doubt over whether it covered cases already before the courts (Malay Mail, 2018b, from search summary), suggests the executive itself regarded enforcement as inconsistent.

**Conclusion.** The process was lawful but open to criticism for institutional overlap and the severity of the first-instance sentence. No court found abuse of process. Certainty: moderate.

## 4. Current status (checked 23 October 2026)

- **Fahmi Reza:** conviction upheld on appeal (Court of Appeal, 5 July 2019); fine reduced to RM10,000 (High Court, 12 November 2018). I did not find a later challenge or pardon. [to verify]
- **Section 233 CMA:** *in force, amended.* The Communications and Multimedia (Amendment) Act 2025 took effect on 11 February 2025. Section 233(1)(a) now reads "grossly offensive" instead of "offensive", keeps the "intent to annoy" language, adds intent to commit offences of fraud or dishonesty, and introduces Explanations 1 to 6 describing prohibited content. The maximum penalty rose from RM50,000 or one year to RM500,000 or two years, with a daily fine of RM5,000 (was RM1,000) after conviction; an offence against a minor carries up to five years (Law Partnership, 2025). Confirm the exact current wording on the Federal Gazette / AGC reprint. [to verify]
- **Constitutional position:** Federal Court, 6 February 2026, upheld "offensive" and "annoy" and set an intent-based threshold (Skrine, 2026). Commentators expect the next dispute to be the meaning of "grossly" (Skrine, 2026).
- **Related regulation:** the Online Safety Act 2025 (in force 1 January 2026) and the Malaysian Media Council Act 2025 (in force 14 June 2025) are part of the wider landscape (from the repository's status file; not independently re-checked).

## 5. Discussion: from law in the books to law in action

### 5.1 Why the state regulates here

Malaysia justifies s. 233 mainly on public order and morality (Art. 10(2)(a)) and, since 2025, on online harm and fraud. The regulator is a state command-and-control agency (MCMC) combined with prosecution in ordinary courts. The Fahmi case shows the justification stretching. A caricature of a prime minister does not obviously engage public order. The interest actually served is protecting a political office from ridicule, which the Constitution addresses through defamation, not the network-facilities offence.

### 5.2 Theoretical lens

**Normative press theories (Siebert, Peterson & Schramm, 1956).** Under the authoritarian model the press serves the state and criticism of rulers is punishable; under the libertarian model the state does not punish opinion. Malaysia fits a hybrid: rights are constitutionally guaranteed but restrictions are broad and discretionary. The model fits the 2016 to 2019 phase, in which an executive-linked regulator prosecuted mockery of the executive. It fits poorly after 2025 to 2026, when courts asserted review under Article 4(1), rejected an "originalist" immunity for Article 10(2) laws, and protected satire in principle (Skrine, 2026). The typology also dates from a Cold War context and treats the press as institutions, so it says little about a single designer posting on Facebook.

**Habermas's public sphere (1989).** Satire is one way in which citizens contribute to critical debate about those in power, and a prosecution that deters it narrows the sphere. The lens fits because the image belonged to a wider protest culture about the leadership of the day. It fits poorly because social media platforms are commercially owned, algorithmic spaces, and a clown image is affective rather than "rational-critical" in the Habermasian sense.

### 5.3 Comparative analysis

I chose the United Kingdom because s. 127(1) of the Communications Act 2003 is the closest textual cousin of s. 233 ("grossly offensive") and remains in force; India because its Supreme Court struck down a comparable online-speech offence; and the ICCPR as the international benchmark. Case citations for the comparators are from general knowledge and were not re-verified in this session. [citations to verify]

Malaysia sits between the UK, which keeps a "grossly offensive" offence but narrows it by case law (the closest model for the 2025 wording), and India, which removed the offence. Malaysia's route is judicial narrowing rather than repeal, resting on intent, which is also the UK courts' main safety valve. The difference is institutional: the UK prosecutor is not the regulator whose logo is mocked, and UK courts apply a human-rights statute that Malaysia lacks.

### 5.4 Competing arguments and significance

**The government and the Federal Court's case.** Online speech can cause real harm, particularly in a plural society. Targeted harassment, doxxing and hate speech need a legal response, and a provision limited to intentional annoyance, read down by the courts, is a proportionate tool. Changing "offensive" to "grossly offensive" shows responsiveness (Skrine, 2026; Law Partnership, 2025).

**The civil society and scholarly critique.** Terms such as "offensive" and "annoy" are subjective and invite selective enforcement, and the Fahmi case is the standing example: a satirical image of a leader produced a criminal conviction. Article 19 argues that the 2025 amendments, including an open-ended "hate speech" explanation, fail international standards (Article 19, n.d.), and practitioners describe parts of them as vague and circular (AmerBON, n.d.). Surendran (2018) argued at the time that the High Court decision "sets a dangerous and bad precedent". A sharper point is that the real safeguard has come from the courts, not Parliament: the legislature raised the maximum fine tenfold in 2025, while the Federal Court supplied the limits.

**Significance.** The incident shows three things about the trajectory of Malaysian media regulation. First, reform promises (the 2018 moratorium, the plan to amend s. 233) did not stop a conviction being upheld in 2019, so enforcement lagged the rhetoric. Second, the 2025 amendment narrowed one word but raised penalties and widened content categories, so liberalisation is not one-directional. Third, the 2026 Federal Court decision moves the debate from "is the section valid?" to "was the speaker's primary purpose to annoy?", which makes the quality of prosecutorial and judicial fact-finding the main safeguard. Fahmi's own conviction, resting on offensiveness, now looks like the kind of case the Federal Court's test was designed to exclude, though it remains a final judgment.

---

## 6. Conclusion of the report

The prosecution of Fahmi Reza was lawful in the sense that three levels of court upheld it under s. 233(1)(a) as then worded, and the section has since been held constitutional by the Federal Court. The weak point is its application to a political caricature: intent to annoy appears to have been inferred from offensiveness, the inference the Federal Court later rejected, and the enforcement structure (the mocked regulator as prosecutor, two parallel charges) invites criticism. Since 2018 the offence has been redrawn ("grossly offensive", much higher penalties) and judicially narrowed, so a similar caricature today would more probably fall outside it, though the outcome would depend on proof of primary purpose. The main limitation is that I relied on news reports and law-firm summaries rather than the judgments themselves.
