# Public Prosecutor v Azmi Sharom [2015] 6 MLJ 751 (FC): Is the Sedition Act 1948 Constitutional?

*COM741 Media Law and Regulations: Case Report (ILAC with Discussion)*

**How to read this report.** ILAC (Issue, Law, Application, Conclusion) is a doctrinal method: it shows what the law is and how it applies ("law in the books"). It cannot show who the law falls on or whether it is justified ("law in action"). The ILAC blocks therefore come first, and the Discussion then adds theory and comparison.

## Case details

- **Court and date:** Federal Court, Putrajaya, 6 October 2015. Criminal Reference No. 06-5-12-2014(W). Also reported at [2015] 8 CLJ 921 and [2015] 6 AMR 338.
- **Coram:** Arifin Zakaria CJ, Raus Sharif PCA, Zulkefli Ahmad Makinudin CJ (Malaya), Abdull Hamid Embong FCJ and Suriyadi Halim Omar FCJ. Arifin Zakaria CJ delivered the judgment of the court. The CLJ report shows no separate or dissenting judgment.
- **Parties:** Public Prosecutor v Dr Azmi Sharom, an associate professor of law at Universiti Malaya.
- **Procedural history:** The defendant was charged in the Kuala Lumpur Sessions Court under s. 4(1)(b) of the Sedition Act 1948 (uttering seditious words) and alternatively s. 4(1)(c) (publishing a seditious publication). Before trial he sought a reference on the Act's constitutionality. On 5 November 2014 the High Court referred two questions to the Federal Court as a *special case* under s. 84 of the Courts of Judicature Act 1964, a procedure by which a lower court asks the Federal Court to settle a pure question of constitutional law before the trial continues.

## Material facts

Malay Mail Online reported on 14 August 2014 on a political dispute in Selangor. It quoted the defendant as saying, "You don't want a repeat of that, where a secret meeting took place", and "I think what happened in Perak was legally wrong. The best thing to do is do it as legally and transparently as possible" (*PP v Azmi Sharom*, 2015, para 2). A secondary summary (Columbia, 2016) lists a third quotation, but the judgment records two, so this report follows the judgment. The reference was abstract: the court did not decide whether these words were seditious. That was left to the trial.

## ILAC 1: Is the Act valid although Parliament did not enact it? (the court's "second question")

**Issue.** Whether the Sedition Act 1948, a pre-Merdeka (pre-independence) law made by the Federal Legislative Council, is valid and enforceable when Art. 10(2) of the Federal Constitution says "Parliament may by law" restrict free speech.

**Law.** Art. 162(1) continues "existing laws" in force after Merdeka Day, with modifications; Art. 160(2) defines an existing law as one in operation immediately before Merdeka Day; Art. 162(6) lets a court modify such a law to bring it into accord with the Constitution. Post-Merdeka laws, by contrast, are void to the extent of inconsistency under Art. 4(1). The defence relied on *Nordin Salleh* (Supreme Court, 1992), where a State provision restricting association failed because only Parliament may impose Art. 10 restrictions. Status: Act in force.

**Application.** The court took this question first, since a negative answer would end the case (para 5). The Act is "existing law" because it was written law in operation before 31 August 1957 (paras 7, 17-18). It distinguished *Nordin Salleh* on two grounds: the impugned law there was State law and post-Merdeka (para 15). It then read "Parliament may by law" harmoniously with Art. 162; excluding the Act would be "highly restrictive and rigid", and the framers, who had Art. 10(2) in mind, could have excluded it from Art. 162 "in no uncertain terms" but did not (paras 23-25).

On one hand, this is orthodox. Art. 162 would be pointless if pre-Merdeka laws fell on a technicality, and the Act was revised under the Revision of Laws Act 1968. On the other hand, the defence's textual point has force: Art. 10(2) and 10(4) assume Parliament's own judgment of necessity, which no Parliament made for this Act. The court's answer is that the remedy for a rights-offending existing law is modification, not nullity (para 25, citing *Assa Singh*). Notably, the court modified nothing; it found none needed, so the answer on this question rests on the answer to the next two.

**Conclusion.** Yes. Art. 162 saves the Act as existing law, so it is valid and enforceable. This holding is firm and binds lower courts.

## ILAC 2: Will courts review whether an Art. 10(2) restriction is "reasonable"? (first question, part one)

**Issue.** Whether a court may require that a restriction on speech be "reasonable", or whether necessity and expediency are for Parliament alone.

**Law.** Art. 10(2)(a) permits restrictions "as [Parliament] deems necessary or expedient" on listed grounds; the word "reasonable" is absent. Art. 4(2)(b) bars challenge on the ground that Parliament did not deem a restriction necessary. *PP v Pung Chen Choon* (Supreme Court, 1994) held that reasonableness does not arise if the law is, in *pith and substance* (its true character), within a listed ground. The Court of Appeal in *Dr Mohd Nasir Hashim* and the Federal Court in *Sivarasa Rasiah* (2010) had read "reasonable" into Art. 10(2) (Bon & Yong, 2017).

**Application.** The defence argued that *Sivarasa* overlooked *Pung Chen Choon* and that the framers deliberately omitted "reasonable". The court agreed: the word was in the Reid Commission draft but dropped by the Working Committee, adopting a dissent by Justice Abdul Hamid (paras 35-36). It called the reasoning in *Mohd Nasir Hashim* "flawed", since borrowing from *Ooi Ah Phua* (on the timing of access to a lawyer) could not justify inserting a word, which "would be rewriting" Art. 10(2) (paras 37-40). The prosecution's position is textual fidelity and respect for the legislature. The critique is that Art. 10 interacts with Art. 8 (equality), so excluding reasonableness while keeping proportionality draws a line that is hard to hold (Bon & Yong, 2017). This is the least stable part of the judgment.

**Conclusion.** No. Courts will not review whether a restriction is reasonable, but will check that it falls within a listed ground. *Sivarasa* is departed from on this point only.

## ILAC 3: Does s. 4(1) pass the proportionality test? (first question, part two)

**Issue.** Whether s. 4(1) of the Act is a permitted and proportionate restriction under Art. 10(2)(a).

**Law.** The court accepted that restrictions are "not without limit" and must pass a three-part proportionality test (from *de Freitas*, Privy Council, 1998): (i) the objective is important enough to limit a right; (ii) the measures are rationally connected to it; (iii) the means impair the right no more than necessary. "Seditious tendency" is defined in s. 3(1)(a)-(f), including disaffection against any Ruler or Government (a), ill will between races or classes (e), and questioning matters protected by Part III and Arts. 152, 153 and 181 (f). Section 3(2) exempts, for example, showing that a Ruler was misled or pointing out errors in a Government. A first offence under s. 4(1) carries up to three years or RM5,000. Art. 10(4) lets Parliament prohibit questioning of the sensitive matters.

**Application.** The court's reasoning is brief (para 43). Section 4(1) is directed at acts with a "seditious tendency" under s. 3(1)(a)-(f), "consistent with art. 10(2)(a) and art. 10(4)", and not "too remote" from the listed objects. It is also "not a total prohibition", given the s. 3(2) exceptions.

The prosecution would say this suffices: each tendency maps onto security, public order or Art. 10(4), and the exceptions protect legitimate criticism. Critics answer that the court never worked through the three limbs separately. It did not ask whether less restrictive means exist, or whether disaffection against "any Government" in s. 3(1)(a) has any real link to public order. The ICJ (cited in Columbia, 2016) adds that sedition is a strict-liability offence where intention is irrelevant, and Article 19 (2015) says the decision defers heavily to the legislature. The court did not address s. 3(3) (intention irrelevant). The Court of Appeal later held s. 3(3) unconstitutional in *Mat Shuhaimi* (2016), but the Federal Court set that aside in 2018 for abuse of process without reaching the merits (Columbia, n.d.).

The facts matter here. Azmi's words were a legal opinion on a constitutional procedure. Because the court did not decide whether they were seditious, the holding does not say whether applying the Act to speech like this is proportionate. That gap separates the law as upheld from the law as used.

**Conclusion.** No. Section 4(1) does not contravene Art. 10(2)(a). The holding is secure as precedent, but the proportionality analysis is thin and open to criticism.

## Outcome and order

First question answered in the negative; second question in the positive. The matter was remitted to the Sessions Court "without any further delay" (para 45).

**Ratio.** (1) Pre-Merdeka laws are saved by Art. 162 although Parliament did not enact them. (2) Courts do not review the reasonableness of an Art. 10(2) restriction, but it must fall within a listed ground and be proportionate. **Obiter** (not needed for the result): the criticism of *Ooi Ah Phua* and the Reid Commission drafting history, although these support the ratio. **Later treatment:** the Attorney General withdrew the charges on 12 February 2016 under Art. 145(3) (Columbia, 2016). *Mat Shuhaimi* tested s. 3(3) but was decided on procedure.

**Status (checked 23 October 2026):** the Act remains in force. Press reports of August 2026 record the Prime Minister's call to abolish colonial-era laws and a DAP call to repeal the Act, but I found no bill [to verify]. The Sedition (Amendment) Act 2015, which raised penalties, was gazetted in 2015, but reports conflict on whether it is in force [to verify]; the penalties above are those quoted in the judgment.

## Discussion

**Why the state regulates here.** The justifications are security and public order and, through s. 3(1)(e)-(f), race and the constitutional bargain in Arts. 152, 153 and 181. The regulator is the state itself: police, the Attorney General (Art. 145(3)) and courts that defer to Parliament. *Azmi Sharom* shows how narrow the courts' role is in this regime.

**Theoretical lens.** Siebert et al.'s (1956) authoritarian theory fits the Act's design, in which speech is tolerated until it threatens rulers or order. It fits poorly with a constitution that entrenches Art. 10 and a court that applies proportionality. Habermas's (1989) public sphere is the second lens: can citizens reason publicly about state power? Azmi's remark was reasoning about a constitutional crisis, so prosecuting it shows how sedition law can chill that sphere. Hallin and Mancini's (2004) typology fits poorly, since it describes Western media systems rather than criminal speech law.

**Comparative analysis.** Singapore is chosen for shared colonial heritage and a recent repeal. The UK is the origin of the offence and has abolished it. India shares the common law and a constitutional text that expressly says "reasonable" restrictions, which Malaysia's court declined to read in.

| Dimension | Malaysia | Singapore | UK (England and Wales, N. Ireland) | India | ICCPR Art. 19 |

Jurisdictions that share Malaysia's heritage have abolished sedition (the UK, Singapore) or are reviewing it in court (India). Malaysia kept it, and *Azmi Sharom* narrowed the courts' own review. Singapore's repeal is not simply a liberal turn, however: its government has other laws to cover the same concerns, so repeal alone does not settle how speech is protected.

**Competing arguments and significance.** The government's case is that the Act protects a plural society from racial and royal provocation, that Parliament alone judges necessity, and that s. 3(2) preserves legitimate criticism. Civil society and scholars reply that the Act's breadth, strict liability and selective use against opponents breach international standards (Article 19, 2015; Columbia, 2016), and that thin proportionality review makes judicial scrutiny formal. Bon and Yong (2017) are more measured: proportionality survived, which lets later courts develop it. The trajectory is mixed. Reform promises and repeal talk sit beside continued enforcement "as long as it is still in force". Parliament, where the court said the decision belonged, still holds the key.

## Conclusion of the report

In a single judgment, the Federal Court held that the Sedition Act 1948 survives Art. 162 and Art. 10(2)(a). Its ratio is deference on reasonableness, qualified by a proportionality test applied briefly. The legal answer is clear. Whether sedition applied to political commentary is proportionate remains open, and Singapore, the UK and India show other systems moving away from the offence.
