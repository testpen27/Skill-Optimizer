# Public Prosecutor v Azmi Sharom [2015] 6 MLJ 751 (FC): Is the Sedition Act 1948 Constitutional?

*COM741 Media Law and Regulations: Case Report (ILAC with Discussion)*

**How to read this report.** ILAC (Issue, Law, Application, Conclusion) is a doctrinal method. It shows what the law is and how it applies, which is "law in the books". It cannot show who the law falls on or whether it is justified, which is "law in action". The ILAC blocks below therefore come first, and the Discussion then moves to theory and comparison.

## Case details

- **Court and date:** Federal Court, Putrajaya, 6 October 2015. Criminal Reference No. 06-5-12-2014(W). Also reported at [2015] 8 CLJ 921 and [2015] 6 AMR 338.
- **Coram:** Arifin Zakaria CJ, Raus Sharif PCA, Zulkefli Ahmad Makinudin CJ (Malaya), Abdull Hamid Embong FCJ and Suriyadi Halim Omar FCJ. Arifin Zakaria CJ delivered the judgment of the court. The CLJ report shows no separate or dissenting judgment.
- **Parties:** Public Prosecutor (plaintiff in the reference) v Dr Azmi Sharom, an associate professor of law at Universiti Malaya (defendant).
- **Procedural history:** The defendant was charged in the Kuala Lumpur Sessions Court under s. 4(1)(b) of the Sedition Act 1948 (uttering seditious words) and, in the alternative, s. 4(1)(c) (publishing a seditious publication). Before trial he asked for the Act's constitutionality to be referred to the High Court. On 5 November 2014 the High Court referred two questions to the Federal Court as a special case under s. 84 of the Courts of Judicature Act 1964. A *special case reference* is a procedure by which a lower court asks the Federal Court to answer a pure question of constitutional law before the trial goes on.

## Material facts

Malay Mail Online reported on 14 August 2014 on the Selangor Menteri Besar dispute. It quoted the defendant as saying, "You don't want a repeat of that, where a secret meeting took place", and "I think what happened in Perak was legally wrong. The best thing to do is do it as legally and transparently as possible" (*PP v Azmi Sharom*, 2015, para 2). The statements concerned the 2009 Perak constitutional crisis. Columbia's Global Freedom of Expression summary lists a third quotation, but the judgment itself records only two, so this report relies on the judgment. The reference was abstract: the court did not decide whether these words were in fact seditious. That was left to the trial.

## ILAC 1: Is the Sedition Act valid, given that Parliament did not enact it? (the court's "second question")

**Issue.** Whether the Sedition Act 1948, a pre-Merdeka (pre-independence) law made by the Federal Legislative Council, is valid and enforceable under the Federal Constitution, given that Art. 10(2) says "Parliament may by law" restrict free speech.

**Law.** Art. 10(1)(a) guarantees free speech; Art. 10(2)(a) allows "Parliament by law" to impose restrictions in the interest of security, public order and other listed grounds. Art. 162(1) continues "existing laws" in force after Merdeka Day, subject to modification. Art. 160(2) defines an existing law as one in operation immediately before Merdeka Day. Under Art. 162(6) a court may modify an existing law to bring it into accord with the Constitution. The contrast is with Art. 4(1), which voids post-Merdeka laws that are inconsistent with the Constitution. The defence relied on *Nordin Salleh* (Supreme Court, 1992), where a Kelantan State Constitution provision restricting association was invalid because only Parliament may restrict Art. 10 rights. Status: Act in force (see Status below).

**Application.** The court took the shorter issue first (para 5), since a negative answer would end the case. Three steps follow. First, the Act is "existing law" because it was written law in operation before 31 August 1957 (paras 7, 17-18). Second, the defence argued that "Parliament may by law" is exclusive. The court distinguished *Nordin Salleh* on two grounds: the impugned law there was State law, and it was post-Merdeka (para 15). Third, the court read Art. 10(2) harmoniously with Art. 162. A reading that excluded the Act would be "highly restrictive and rigid", and the framers, who had Art. 10(2) in mind, could have excluded the Act from Art. 162 "in no uncertain terms" but did not (paras 23-25). The 1946 Working Committee report on continuing existing laws was cited in support (para 26).

On one hand, the reasoning is orthodox. Art. 162 would be pointless if every pre-Merdeka law fell on a technicality, and the Act was later revised under the Revision of Laws Act 1968. On the other hand, the defence's textual point has force. Art. 10(4) and Art. 10(2) speak of Parliament's own judgment of necessity, and the Act's wording on "seditious tendency" was never tested by a Parliament that owed its authority to the 1957 Constitution. The court's answer is that Art. 162 allows modification, and the remedy for a rights-offending pre-Merdeka law is modification, not nullity (para 25, citing *Assa Singh*). Notably, the court modified nothing. It concluded that no modification was needed, which makes the second question depend heavily on the answer to the first.

**Conclusion.** Yes. The Act is saved by Art. 162 as existing law and is valid and enforceable. This is a firm holding of the Federal Court, binding on lower courts.

## ILAC 2: Will the court review whether a restriction is "reasonable"? (first question, part one)

**Issue.** Whether a court may require that a restriction under Art. 10(2)(a) be "reasonable", or whether necessity and expediency are for Parliament alone.

**Law.** Art. 10(2)(a) says restrictions "as it deems necessary or expedient"; the word "reasonable" does not appear. Art. 4(2)(b) provides that a law's validity cannot be questioned on the ground that Parliament did not deem the restriction necessary or expedient. *PP v Pung Chen Choon* [1994] (Supreme Court) held that the reasonableness of the restriction did not arise if the law was in pith and substance (its true character) within a listed Art. 10(2)(a) ground. The Court of Appeal in *Dr Mohd Nasir Hashim* (2006/2007) and the Federal Court in *Sivarasa Rasiah* (2010) had read "reasonable" into Art. 10(2) (see Bon & Yong, 2017). Art. 19(2) of India's Constitution does contain the word "reasonable".

**Application.** The defence asked the court to depart from *Sivarasa* because it overlooked *Pung Chen Choon* and because the framers deliberately omitted "reasonable" (para 35). The court agreed. The word appeared in the Reid Commission draft but was dropped by the Working Committee, following Justice Abdul Hamid's dissent (para 36). It held that the reasoning in *Mohd Nasir Hashim* was "flawed", because borrowing the "as soon as may be" balance from *Ooi Ah Phua* did not justify inserting a word into Art. 10(2), which "would be rewriting" it (paras 37-40). The prosecution's view is that this is a straightforward respect for text and for the separation of powers. The critique, made by Bon and Yong (2017), is that excluding reasonableness ignores the interaction of Art. 10 with Art. 8, and that the court's own proportionality test is hard to distinguish from reasonableness. The court's line between the two is thin, and it is the least stable part of the judgment.

**Conclusion.** No. The court will not insert "reasonable" into Art. 10(2) or review Parliament's view of necessity, but it will check that the law falls within a listed ground. *Sivarasa* is departed from on this point only.

## ILAC 3: Does s. 4(1) pass the proportionality test? (first question, part two)

**Issue.** Whether s. 4(1) of the Sedition Act, which criminalises seditious acts, words and publications, is a permitted restriction under Art. 10(2)(a) and proportionate.

**Law.** The court accepted from *Sivarasa* that restrictions are "not without limit" and must pass a three-part proportionality test (from *de Freitas* [1998] UKPC 30, Privy Council): (i) the objective is important enough to justify limiting a right; (ii) the measures are rationally connected to it; (iii) the means impair the right no more than necessary. Section 3(1) defines "seditious tendency" in six paragraphs (a)-(f), including tendencies to excite disaffection against any Ruler or Government (a), to promote ill will between races or classes (e), and to question matters protected by Part III, Arts. 152, 153 and 181 (f). Section 3(2) exempts, for example, showing that a Ruler was misled, or pointing out errors in a Government. Section 4(1) carries up to three years' imprisonment or RM5,000 for a first offence. Art. 10(4) allows Parliament to prohibit questioning of the sensitive matters in s. 3(1)(f).

**Application.** The court's application is brief (para 43). It reasoned that s. 4(1) is directed at acts having a "seditious tendency" as defined in s. 3(1)(a)-(f), which is "consistent with art. 10(2)(a) and art. 10(4)", and that the connection to the listed objects is not "too remote". It also said s. 4(1) is not a total prohibition because of the s. 3(2) exceptions.

The prosecution would say that this is enough. Each tendency in s. 3(1) maps onto security, public order or Art. 10(4), and the exceptions leave room for legitimate criticism. The defence and critics would answer that the court never worked through the three limbs separately. It did not ask whether less restrictive means exist, or whether s. 3(1)(a), disaffection against "any Government", has any real tie to public order. The critics (ICJ, cited in Columbia, 2016) add that sedition is a strict-liability offence in which intention is irrelevant, and Article 19 (2015) argues that the decision defers heavily to the legislature. The court also did not consider the s. 3(3) intention point; the Court of Appeal later held s. 3(3) unconstitutional in *Mat Shuhaimi* (2016), but the Federal Court reversed that on procedural grounds in 2018 without reaching the merits.

Compare the facts. The words in this case were an opinion on constitutional procedure. Whether they had a seditious tendency was not decided, so the holding says nothing on whether the Act's application to speech like this is proportionate. That is the main gap between the law as upheld and the law as used.

**Conclusion.** No. Section 4(1) does not contravene Art. 10(2)(a). The reasoning on proportionality is thin, so the holding is secure as precedent but open to criticism.

## Outcome and order

The first question was answered in the negative and the second in the positive. The matter was remitted to the Sessions Court "without any further delay" (para 45).

**Ratio.** (1) Pre-Merdeka laws are saved by Art. 162 even though Parliament did not enact them. (2) Courts do not review the reasonableness of an Art. 10(2) restriction, but a restriction must fall within a listed ground and be proportionate. **Obiter** (not necessary to the result): the court's remarks on *Ooi Ah Phua* and the Reid Commission drafting history, though these are persuasive of future reasoning. **Later treatment:** the Attorney General withdrew the charges on 12 February 2016 using Art. 145(3) (Columbia, 2016). *Mat Shuhaimi* (CA 2016, FC 2018) tested s. 3(3) but was decided on abuse of process.

**Status checked 23 October 2026:** the Sedition Act 1948 remains in force. Press reports in August 2026 record the Prime Minister saying colonial-era laws should go, and a DAP call for repeal, but I found no bill [to verify]. The Sedition (Amendment) Act 2015, which raised penalties, was gazetted in 2015, but reports conflict on whether it has been brought into force [to verify], so the s. 4 penalties above are those quoted in the judgment.

## Discussion

**Why the state regulates here.** The justification is national security, public order, and, through s. 3(1)(e)-(f), race and religion and the "social contract" of Arts. 152, 153 and 181. The regulator is the state itself, in the form of police, the Attorney General (Art. 145(3)), and courts that defer to Parliament. *Azmi Sharom* shows that the courts' role in this regime is narrow.

**Theoretical lens.** Siebert, Peterson and Schramm's (1956) authoritarian theory fits the Act's design: speech is tolerated until it threatens rulers or order. It fits poorly with the modern Malaysian setting, where courts apply a proportionality test and the Art. 10 right is constitutionally entrenched, and where the Act is enforced selectively rather than as a general licence. Habermas's (1989) public sphere is the second lens. It asks whether citizens can reason in public about state power. Azmi's remark was exactly this kind of reasoning about a constitutional crisis, so the decision shows how sedition law can chill the public sphere. Hallin and Mancini (2004) is a poor fit, since it concerns Western media systems and does not address criminal speech law.

**Comparative analysis.** Singapore is chosen for shared colonial heritage and the same 1948-era text. The UK is the source of the offence. India is chosen for a common law constitution with an express "reasonable restriction" wording, which Malaysia's court rejected.

The table shows a pattern. Jurisdictions that share Malaysia's heritage have either abolished sedition (the UK, Singapore) or are subjecting it to court review (India). Malaysia keeps it and, in *Azmi Sharom*, narrowed the court's own review. Singapore's repeal is not a liberal turn, however. Its government replaced sedition with other laws, so the comparison supports the point that repeal alone does not settle how free speech is protected.

**Competing arguments and significance.** The government's case is that the Act protects a plural society from racial and royal provocation, that Parliament alone judges necessity, and that s. 3(2) preserves legitimate criticism. Civil society and scholars reply that the Act's breadth, its strict-liability reach and its selective use against opponents breach international standards (Article 19, 2015; Columbia, 2016), and that the court's thin proportionality analysis made judicial review largely formal. Bon and Yong (2017) take a more measured view: the court kept proportionality alive, which gives later courts room to develop it. The trajectory is mixed. The reform promises of successive governments, including the current repeal talk, sit beside continued enforcement "as long as it is still in force". *Azmi Sharom* therefore leaves the Act's future with Parliament, which is where the court said it belonged.

## Conclusion of the report

The Federal Court held unanimously that the Sedition Act 1948 survives Art. 162 and Art. 10(2)(a). Its ratio is deference on reasonableness, qualified by a proportionality test that was applied briefly. The legal answer is clear, but the decision leaves open whether sedition as applied to political commentary is proportionate, and comparison with Singapore, the UK and India shows that other systems have moved away from the offence.
