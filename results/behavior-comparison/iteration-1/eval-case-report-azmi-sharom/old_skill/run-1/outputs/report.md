# Case Report: *Public Prosecutor v Azmi Sharom* [2015] 6 MLJ 751 (FC)

COM741 Media Law and Regulations. Status of laws checked 23 October 2026.

## 1. Case details

- **Court and date:** Federal Court of Malaysia, 6 October 2015. Criminal Reference No. 06-5-12-2014(W). Reported also as [2015] 6 AMR 338 and [2015] 8 CLJ 921.
- **Coram:** Arifin Zakaria CJ (who delivered the judgment), Raus Sharif PCA, Zulkefli Ahmad Makinudin CJ (Malaya), Abdull Hamid Embong FCJ and Suriyadi Halim Omar FCJ. The decision was unanimous; no dissent has been reported (Article 19, 2015).
- **Parties:** Public Prosecutor (prosecution) and Azmi Sharom, then an Associate Professor of Law at Universiti Malaya (accused).
- **Procedural history:** Azmi was charged on 2 September 2014 in the Kuala Lumpur Sessions Court under s. 4(1)(b) of the Sedition Act 1948 (Act 15) (uttering seditious words), with an alternative charge under s. 4(1)(c) (publishing a seditious publication). He challenged the Act's constitutionality. Two questions were then referred to the Federal Court under s. 84 of the Courts of Judicature Act 1964: (a) whether s. 4(1) contravenes Article 10(2) of the Federal Constitution and is void under Article 4(1); and (b) whether the Act is a valid and enforceable Act under the Constitution (Bon & Yong, 2017).

## 2. Material facts

On 14 August 2014 Malay Mail Online published an article on a political impasse in Selangor, where the removal of the Menteri Besar (Chief Minister) was in question. Azmi was quoted comparing the situation to the 2009 Perak crisis. He said, among other things, that "what happened in Perak was legally wrong" and that "a vote by the house of representative should be done in the house of representative" (Columbia GFoE, n.d.). The prosecution treated these remarks as seditious. Azmi's counsel argued that the Act was never enacted by Parliament but by the colonial Federal Legislative Council, and so could not validly restrict speech under Article 10(2). The wording of the statement is taken from a secondary source; the judgment itself was not retrieved [to verify against the eJudgment text].

## 3. ILAC

### Issue 1: Is the Sedition Act 1948 a valid and enforceable law when Article 10(2) says only "Parliament" may restrict speech "by law", yet the Act was made by the pre-Merdeka Federal Legislative Council?

**Law.** Article 10(1)(a) guarantees every citizen freedom of speech and expression. Article 10(2)(a) allows "Parliament" to restrict it "by law", as it "deems necessary or expedient" in the interest of security, friendly relations, public order or morality, and for specified purposes such as defamation or incitement to an offence. Article 4(1) makes any law inconsistent with the Constitution void. Article 162 continues "existing laws" (laws in force before Merdeka, 31 August 1957) in force, subject to modifications to bring them into conformity with the Constitution. The defence relied on *Nordin Salleh v Kerajaan Negeri Kelantan* [1993] 2 AMR 3086 (SC), where a restriction on the freedom of association was struck down partly because Article 10(2) names "Parliament". The Sedition Ordinance 1948 was later retitled the Sedition Act 1948 in the 1969 law revision. Status: **in force** (see Section 5).

**Application.** The defence argued, on one hand, that Article 10(2) is textually exclusive: only Parliament, a body that did not exist in 1948, can restrict speech, so a colonial ordinance cannot qualify. It also argued that the Yang di-Pertuan Agong's power to modify existing laws under Article 162 does not reach laws that restrict fundamental liberties. On the other hand, the State argued that Article 162 would be pointless if every pre-Merdeka law had to be re-enacted before it could bind, so the Act must be treated as if Parliament had made it. The Federal Court preferred the State's reading. It held that "Parliament may by law" must not be read in a "highly restrictive and rigid" manner and must be read harmoniously with Article 162. It reasoned that the framers intended existing law to "continue to be valid and enforceable" on Merdeka Day (Columbia GFoE, n.d.; Bon & Yong, 2017). An existing law is invalid only if it cannot be modified to accord with the Constitution. The *ratio decidendi* (the binding legal reason) is that Article 162 saves existing laws such as the Sedition Act from the objection that Parliament did not make them. The court's handling of *Nordin Salleh* is reported only briefly in my sources [to verify].

**Conclusion.** Yes. The Act is valid and enforceable because Article 162 preserves it. This is the settled position for a Federal Court ruling, though critics say it sidesteps the text of Article 10(2).

### Issue 2: Does s. 4(1) of the Sedition Act 1948 impose a restriction that falls outside Article 10(2)(a), because it is disproportionate to the aim of protecting security or public order, and is it therefore void under Article 4(1)?

**Law.** Section 4(1) makes it an offence to utter seditious words or publish a seditious publication. A first offence carries a fine of up to RM5,000, up to three years' imprisonment, or both. Section 3(1) defines "seditious tendency" broadly, covering a tendency to bring into hatred or contempt, or excite disaffection against, a Ruler or government, or to promote ill will between races or classes. Section 3(2) lists exceptions, such as pointing out errors in government or advocating lawful change; I paraphrase this from memory and from Columbia GFoE (n.d.), so it should be checked against the current official text [to verify]. Under s. 3(3), intention is not an element of the offence (*mens rea* is the mental element, such as intention); this was later challenged in *Mat Shuhaimi Shafiei v Kerajaan Malaysia* (CA, 2016). In *Sivarasa Rasiah v Badan Peguam Malaysia* [2010] 2 AMR 301 (FC), Gopal Sri Ram FCJ held that restrictions must be "reasonable" and "proportionate". The Court of Appeal in *PP v Yuneswaran Ramaraj* [2015] 6 AMR 271 had just called the "reasonable" reading *obiter dicta* (remarks not essential to the decision) and followed *PP v Pung Chen Choon* [1994] 1 AMR 689 (SC), under which Parliament, not the courts, judges reasonableness. The Sedition (Amendment) Act 2015 widened the Act; it was passed in April 2015, but I could not confirm its gazetted commencement date [to verify].

**Application.** Two elements matter: (1) whether the court may review a restriction's reasonableness, and (2) whether s. 4(1) passes the proportionality test. On the first, the Federal Court departed from *Sivarasa*: the courts do not decide whether Parliament's restriction is "reasonable", because the Reid Commission's word "reasonable" had been left out of the final Article 10(2). The Article 19 critique reads this as heavy deference, saying the court "considerably limit[ed]" its own oversight (Article 19, 2015). On the second, it still held that restrictions "must pass the proportionality test". That test asks whether the objective is important enough to limit a right, whether the means are rationally connected to it, and whether they impair the right no more than necessary (Bon & Yong, 2017). The court found that s. 4(1) does not amount to a "total prohibition" of speech because s. 3(2) preserves some lawful criticism, so it was not disproportionate. The State's strongest point is that s. 3(2) leaves room for criticism aimed at lawful reform. The defence's strongest point is that "seditious tendency" is vague, needs no intent and no likelihood of violence, and is open to selective enforcement. The International Commission of Jurists and ARTICLE 19 make the vagueness argument; the Federal Court, as reported, did not engage with it in depth [to verify against the judgment]. The *ratio* is that s. 4(1) passes proportionality because of the s. 3(2) exceptions. The court's remarks on what remains of *Sivarasa* may be treated as *obiter* by later courts, since only proportionality was needed to decide the case, but the departure on "reasonable" shaped the reasoning, so classifying it is contestable.

**Conclusion.** No. The Federal Court held that s. 4(1) does not run counter to Article 10(2)(a) (Columbia GFoE, n.d.). The holding is firm as a matter of precedent, but its reasoning on proportionality is thin and has been criticised as deferential.

## 4. Outcome and order

The Federal Court answered both referred questions in favour of the Act's validity and remitted the case to the Sessions Court for trial. The Attorney General discontinued the prosecution under Article 145(3) on 12 February 2016, citing "the interest of justice" (Columbia GFoE, n.d.). One source gives December 2016 for the discontinuance and another reports a later discharge; I follow the February 2016 date from the sourced Star quotation [to verify].

## 5. Discussion

### Why the state regulates here, and how

The justification is security, public order and, in the Sedition Act's wording, harmony between races and respect for the Rulers. The regulator is the criminal courts working through state command-and-control: the Attorney General controls prosecution under Article 145(3), as the 2016 discontinuance shows. Article 10(4), which allows laws against questioning sensitive matters, sits behind this framing.

### Theoretical lenses

*Authoritarian press theory* (Siebert et al., 1956) fits the Act reasonably well: the state protects itself and the ruler from criticism, and speech is a privilege conditioned on loyalty. The fit is imperfect, because that theory describes pre-democratic licensing regimes, whereas Malaysia has competitive elections, a judiciary that heard a full constitutional challenge, and a stated proportionality test. Habermas's (1989) *public sphere* gives a better fit for this case. Azmi's remarks were a rational-critical comment on a constitutional dispute, exactly the type of talk the public sphere needs, and the Act's open "tendency" test chills it. It fits poorly where the Act protects race relations, since Habermas assumes equal access to debate, not a multi-ethnic settlement that places certain questions off limits. Hallin and Mancini's (2004) models were not used because they were built for Western democracies.

### Comparative analysis

I chose Singapore (shared colonial heritage and a similar statute, now repealed), the United Kingdom (the origin of the common-law offence, now abolished) and India (a colonial-era sedition law in a constitutional democracy with active judicial review). Malaysia is not a party to the ICCPR (Columbia GFoE, n.d.; Article 19, 2015), so the final column is a benchmark, not a binding obligation. The comparison shows that Malaysia keeps what the three comparators have either abolished (UK, Singapore) or narrowed or suspended (India): an offence defined by tendency, with no intent requirement. It also shows the most important difference in safeguards. Indian courts apply a textual "reasonable restriction" test, whereas the Federal Court in *Azmi Sharom* declined to read "reasonable" into Article 10(2), and so removed the judge's strongest tool.

### Competing arguments and significance

The government's case is that Malaysia is a plural society where the 1969 racial riots are a reminder of what speech can do, that Article 10(2) and (4) were drafted as part of the constitutional bargain, and that the Act is used sparingly. The Home Minister said in July 2026 that it will be enforced "as long as it is still in force" and used "sparingly" for matters touching the Royal Institution (reported in the search sources; primary source not checked) [to verify]. The critics' case, put by ARTICLE 19 and the ICJ, is that the Act is vague, needs no intent, and was applied selectively against opponents in a surge of cases from 2014. The ICJ counted a sharp rise after 2014 (Columbia GFoE, n.d.). Both positions are serious. The reformist reply is that the proportionality test is a step forward; the sceptical reply is that applying it to a no-intent offence makes it formal only.

The case shows a trajectory in which the courts accepted the legislature's primacy over speech restrictions. Reform promises have not been realised: repeal was promised in 2012 and again by successive governments, and as of the latest reports found (August 2026) Prime Minister Anwar Ibrahim has again said colonial laws should be abolished while the Act remains in force. The Court of Appeal in *Mat Shuhaimi* (2016) held s. 3(3) unconstitutional, but the Federal Court set that decision aside on procedural grounds in January 2018 without ruling on the merits. This means the intent question remains unsettled in the courts.

## 6. Conclusion of the report

In *Azmi Sharom*, the Federal Court held that the Sedition Act 1948 survives constitutional challenge by two routes: Article 162 treats the colonial-era Act as valid existing law, and s. 4(1) satisfies a proportionality test that, on the court's approach, stops short of reviewing reasonableness. The ruling protects the Act's validity but leaves its breadth, the absence of an intent element and its selective use as live questions, which reform, not litigation, has so far failed to settle. For media law students, the case is best read as an example of judicial deference in a system where the legislature, not the court, defines the limits of speech.
