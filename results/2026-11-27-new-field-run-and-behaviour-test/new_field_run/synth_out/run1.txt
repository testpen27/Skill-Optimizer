Across the eight papers reviewed on physical activity and depression, explicit theoretical grounding is sparse: only Self-Determination Theory is named as an anchoring framework, in one cross-sectional student survey, while one qualitative study offers behavioral activation as a possible interpretation of its findings, and the remaining papers name no theory, relying instead on reporting guidelines such as CONSORT, PRISMA 2020 and PRISMA-P. Methodologically the set is varied, comprising three systematic reviews and meta-analyses of randomized trials (two of them still protocols without results), two cross-sectional online surveys and one prospective observational study using self-report instruments such as the IPAQ, PHQ-9 and Beck Depression Inventory, one pilot mixed-methods randomized trial of an online exercise program and one reflexive thematic analysis of inpatient interviews. All eight papers appear in Q1 journals, although only one is ranked in the Psychiatry and Mental Health category, with the others placed as Q1 in multidisciplinary, psychology, medicine or biomedical engineering categories, so no paper in the set is Unranked/Unavailable. The findings broadly agree that greater physical activity or structured exercise accompanies fewer depressive symptoms, as seen in the pooled reduction in rheumatoid arthritis trials, the pre-to-post decrease after online exercise, the PHQ-9 improvement among underactive platform users and the mood improvements inpatients described. They differ in the strength and directness of that link, however, since the student surveys report only very weak or small indirect associations, the pilot trial found no change in habitual activity, anxiety or loneliness, and the qualitative and review authors stress that their evidence reflects subjective experience or is promising but not definitive.
