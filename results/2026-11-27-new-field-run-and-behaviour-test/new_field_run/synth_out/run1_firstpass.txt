Across the eight papers reviewed on physical activity and depression, explicit theoretical framing is sparse: only Self-Determination Theory is named as an anchoring framework (Chen, 2026), behavioral activation is offered as one possible interpretation alongside a contextualist analytic perspective (Bystad, 2026), and the remaining studies name no theory, with Coletta (2024) guided only by the CONSORT pilot-trial extension. Methodologically, the set leans towards quantitative and review designs, comprising two cross-sectional online surveys, a prospective observational study, a pilot mixed-methods RCT, a reflexive thematic analysis of inpatient interviews, one completed PRISMA 2020 meta-analysis of randomized trials and two registered or PRISMA-P protocols for further meta-analyses, with depression commonly measured by self-report instruments such as the PHQ-9 and Beck Depression Inventory and physical activity by the IPAQ or accelerometry. All eight papers appear in Q1 journals, although only one (Bystad, 2026) is ranked in Psychiatry and Mental Health, while the rest are Q1 in Multidisciplinary, Psychology, Medicine or Biomedical Engineering categories. The findings broadly agree that greater physical activity or structured exercise accompanies fewer depressive symptoms, from a moderate-certainty pooled effect in rheumatoid arthritis (SMD = −0.49) to reduced symptoms after online exercise among older adults and after increased activity on a digital mental health platform. They differ in strength and mechanism, however: the survey studies report small or very weak associations, including an indirect-only effect through positive mental health (Chen, 2026) and few significant correlations among Polish and Portuguese students (Zalewska, 2023), and the qualitative inpatient study cautions that perceived mood benefits reflect subjective experience rather than evidence of efficacy.
